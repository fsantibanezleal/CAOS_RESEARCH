\begin{abstract}
A seven-vertex instance announced in July 2026 refutes Goemans' conjecture that a
feasible fractional single-source flow can be rounded to an unsplittable flow that
loads no arc beyond $x_a+\dmax$ and costs no more than the fractional flow. We
verify that instance, and the eleven instances that currently hold the lower bounds
on the violation constant, by exact rational enumeration with a library written from
the statement of the conjecture, without running any verifier of their authors. The
seven-vertex instance forces a violation of exactly $\tfrac{16}{15}\dmax$. We prove
that a separation linear program decides whether any nonnegative cost vector makes an
instance a counterexample, that its optimum on the seven-vertex instance is
$\tfrac27$ and is attained by the published prices, and that no single-terminal
instance is a counterexample. A cutting-plane evaluator computes the frontier value,
the violation forced by the best prices, exactly on instances with \nkroutings\
routings. The eleven record instances of Nikolenko and De la Fuente reproduce their
claimed values to the last digit; the two 17-terminal instances are non-planar and
the others planar, each decided by a checked certificate; and re-pricing gains
nothing on any of them. Exact checks of the symmetric three-terminal family of
Muffatti, with forced violation $\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$, and of Afrasyab's
odd-cycle family on $C_5$, whose \nsixtotal\ members all have frontier value at most 1,
complete the computations. The theorems of the sources are not re-proved, and the
two-terminal case remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A single-source unsplittable flow sends the whole demand of each terminal along one path from a common
source. Given a digraph with a source $s$, terminals $T$ with demands $d_t$, and a fractional flow $x$ that
routes $d_t$ to each terminal $t$, the question is how well $x$ can be rounded to one path per terminal. Dinitz,
Garg and Goemans proved that a rounding exists that overloads no arc by more than the largest demand
$\dmax$, that is, with load at most $x_a+\dmax$ on every arc $a$ \cite{DGG99}. Goemans conjectured that the same
rounding can be made to cost no more than $x$ under any nonnegative arc costs. The conjecture appears in
the work of Skutella \cite{Sku02} on the min-cost version of the problem, it was strengthened by Morell and
Skutella \cite{MS22}, and it was a standing open problem until 2026: as late as October 2025 the primary
literature recorded that it remains wide open whether it holds even with a violation of $O(\dmax)$
\cite{STVZ25}. The conjecture had been proved for demands that are multiples of one another
\cite{Sku02}, for series-parallel digraphs \cite{MSW25}, and, at the doubled violation $2\dmax$, for planar
graphs \cite{TVZ24}.

On 22 and 23 July 2026 a seven-vertex counterexample was announced publicly by Rybin \cite{Ryb26}, outside
peer review, and a formal proof of its defining inequality was later added to the Archive of Formal Proofs
\cite{AFP26}. In the following weeks the area produced a record of lower bounds on the violation constant: Nikolenko
published three preprints \cite{Nik1,Nik2,Nik3} with a planar four-terminal family, a 17-terminal instance that
De la Fuente refined \cite{DLF26}, and a planar instance, and he proved that the value $\tfrac98$ is the exact
supremum of the symmetric three-terminal template underlying the counterexample \cite{Nik1}. Muffatti \cite{Muf26}
gave a family on the topology of the counterexample whose forced violation approaches $\tfrac98$,
Afrasyab \cite{Afr26} realised any odd cycle of conflicts in an acyclic single-source instance, and Bérczi, Liu,
Reis and Tarnawski \cite{BLRT26} disproved the cost-free two-sided conjecture of Morell and Skutella. Every
new value in this series is certified by its author's own verifier, and none had been recomputed by an
instrument written independently of the series.

This paper supplies that recomputation and the exact instruments it needs. We write the instance model
and the decision procedure from the statement of the conjecture, in exact rational arithmetic, and use them
to verify the seven-vertex instance (Section~\ref{sec:seven}) and the eleven record instances
(Section~\ref{sec:records}). The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an independent exact verification of the seven-vertex instance by complete path enumeration, a battery of
consistency tests against every proved result in the area, and the exact violation it forces,
$\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=\tfrac{16}{15}$, which the announcement did not state;
\item the separation linear program, with its proof in both directions, which removes the cost vector from any
search; its optimum $\tfrac27$ on the seven-vertex instance, attained by the published prices; a theorem that no
single-terminal instance is a counterexample, and a necessary condition at two terminals;
\item the frontier value, the violation that the best prices force on an instance, computed exactly by an
anti-cycling simplex and by a cutting-plane evaluator that decides instances with $\nkroutings$ routings;
\item the verification of the eleven record instances of Nikolenko and De la Fuente: every claimed value is
reproduced to the last digit, planarity or non-planarity is decided by a certificate that a separate checker
accepts, and the frontier value equals the claimed value on every instance, so no re-pricing gains anything;
\item exact checks of two published families: Muffatti's symmetric family, with forced violation exactly
$\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$, and Afrasyab's odd-cycle family on $C_5$, on which the frontier value never exceeds 1
over \nsixtotal\ members.
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes no claim of a new bound and none of priority. The counterexample is Rybin's; the current lower
bounds on the violation constant are Nikolenko's and De la Fuente's; the value $\tfrac98$ as a supremum is
Nikolenko's, and the family that approaches it exactly is Muffatti's; the realisation of odd cycles is
Afrasyab's. What is ours is the verification, the exact instruments, the certificates and the optimality of the
prices on each instance. The theorems of the three preprints (the suprema, the class results, the
exact-mean characterisation) are not re-proved here; only the finite instances are decided. Section~\ref{sec:related}
places the sources, Section~\ref{sec:problem} fixes the objects and the evidence marks, Section~\ref{sec:methods}
gives the procedures, Section~\ref{sec:theory} their proofs, and Sections~\ref{sec:design} to \ref{sec:ablation}
the computations, their results and their controls.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{The single-source problem and its approximation.} Kleinberg introduced single-source unsplittable
flow in his thesis \cite{Kle96}; the problem contains Partition, Bin Packing and makespan scheduling on parallel
machines as special cases. Dinitz, Garg and Goemans \cite{DGG99} gave the load bound $x_a+\dmax$ with an
augmentation along residual cycles, and Morell and Skutella \cite{MS22} later gave a one-page proof of the same
bound and of its covering analogue $y_a\ge x_a-\dmax$. The proof assumes a feasible fractional flow and no costs;
it leaves open the simultaneous cost guarantee, which is the conjecture studied here. Kolliopoulos and Stein
\cite{KS02} and Skutella \cite{Sku02} treat the optimisation versions, with minimum cost for the latter; Du and
Kolliopoulos \cite{DK05} implemented and tested several of those algorithms. Skutella proves Goemans'
conjecture when the demands are integer multiples of one another.

\paragraph{Relaxations and the general problem.} For several commodities with their own sources and
destinations, Raghavan and Thompson \cite{RT87,Rag88} round fractional solutions randomly and bound the
congestion; Kolliopoulos \cite{Kol07} surveys the area. Baier, K\"ohler and Skutella \cite{BKS05} relax
unsplittability to routing each commodity along at most $k$ paths, and single-source $k$-splittable flows are
studied by Kolliopoulos \cite{Kol05}, by Koch, Skutella and Spenke \cite{KSS08} and by Salazar and Skutella
\cite{SS09}. These works concern approximation algorithms and relaxations of unsplittability; none of the sources read here
states a result on the exact constant for a simultaneous cost bound. Shepherd and Vetta
\cite{SV15} prove that maximum single-sink unsplittable flow is hard to approximate within $n^{1/2-\epsilon}$
without the no-bottleneck assumption; their regime is routability, and the result neither supports nor
obstructs the rounding conjecture.

\paragraph{Convex combinations and the two-sided conjectures.} Martens, Salazar and Skutella \cite{MSS07} relate
the cost version of the conjecture to a statement that $x$ is a convex combination of unsplittable flows that
satisfy the load bound; the easy direction of the equivalence is used in Section~\ref{sec:seven}. Morell and
Skutella \cite{MS22} state two conjectures: a two-sided bound $x_a-\dmax\le y_a\le x_a+\dmax$ on an acyclic
digraph (their Conjecture~1), and the same with arbitrary costs and $c(y)\le c(x)$ (their Conjecture~2), a
strengthening of Goemans' conjecture. They prove the two-sided statement with a convex combination when the demands
are multiples of one another. The labels used by the later literature are those of Swamy, Traub, Vargas Koch and
Zenklusen \cite{STVZ25}: Conjectures~1.2 (Goemans), 1.3 and 1.4 (Morell and Skutella without and with costs) and 1.5
(two-sided with $2\dmax$ and costs). Swamy and coauthors show that Conjecture~1.3 implies Conjecture~1.5
(their Theorem~1.6) and give a weighted ring-loading bound with constant $\tfrac{13}{5}$.

\paragraph{Graph classes.} Traub, Vargas Koch and Zenklusen \cite{TVZ24} prove the cost-preserving guarantee with
violation $2\dmax$, and the two-sided bounds without costs, for planar graphs; they note that measuring the
violation against $x$ is a worst-case assumption in which capacities equal $x$. Majthoub Almoghrabi, Skutella and
Warode \cite{MSW25} prove the cost conjecture for series-parallel digraphs in the convex-combination form and with
deviation strictly below $\dmax$. The two-layer case follows from rounding techniques of Lenstra, Shmoys and Tardos
\cite{LST90}, as cited in \cite{TVZ24}. These theorems fix the consistency battery of Section~\ref{sec:seven}: a
counterexample must lie outside every class on which the conjecture is proved.

\paragraph{After the refutation.} Rybin's announcement \cite{Ryb26} gives the seven-vertex instance with demands
$(15,10,15)$ and fractional cost 58 against a minimum of 60 for every congestion-good routing; the formal entry
\cite{AFP26} proves that inequality in Isabelle/HOL for the nine arcs and the demands of that instance, and
does not formalise the forced violation $\tfrac{16}{15}$, the separation optimum or the acyclicity corollaries.
Nikolenko's first preprint \cite{Nik1} gives a planar four-terminal family with supremum
$(299-41\sqrt{41})/32$ and proves that $\tfrac98$ is the exact supremum of the symmetric three-terminal
template; the second \cite{Nik2} gives the 17-terminal instance and upper bounds below 2 on a family of extremal
cells; the third \cite{Nik3} proves a mixture characterisation of the optimal constant by separation, exact-mean
decompositions, a planar instance with value $1.17353531974518$, and an open problem on the factor-two merger
constant. The repository of the series \cite{NikRepo} holds the verifiers and certificates on which the
claimed values rest, and De la Fuente's refinement \cite{DLF26} of the 17-terminal instance was merged into it.
Muffatti \cite{Muf26} proves in a research note that the topology of the counterexample, with demands
$(1,\tfrac34,1)$, forces $\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$; Afrasyab \cite{Afr26} shows that every finite loopless
independence system is realised by the zero-cost choices of an acyclic single-source instance and gives an exact
cost-preserving threshold $1-bq$ on a uniform odd-cycle family. Bérczi, Liu, Reis and Tarnawski \cite{BLRT26}
disprove Conjecture~1.3 of \cite{STVZ25} for every $k\ge4$ with a forced two-sided deviation
$(\tfrac98-\tfrac3{8k})\dmax$; their instance is cost-free, so the refutation does not depend on the 2026
instance. Cherniavskii, Kupavskii, Moiseev and Shekhovtsov \cite{CKMS26} prove a cost-preserving rounding with load
$L_e(x)+O(\rho_{\max}\sqrt{D})$ on directed multigraphs whose admissible paths have at most $D$ hops.

\paragraph{Where this paper stands.} Each of these sources states results about families, classes or constants, and
certifies finite instances with the verifier of its author. This paper does none of the proving; it decides the finite
instances again, with an instrument that was written before the series and from the statement of the conjecture,
adds the certificates of planarity that the papers do not give, and reports whether the prices of each instance can be
improved. The seven-vertex instance and the instances of Muffatti and Afrasyab are also decided exactly under their
own cost vectors and under the best cost vector.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Instances, routings and the two goodness conditions}
\label{sec:instances}

An instance is a tuple $(G,s,T,d,x)$ with $G=(V,A)$ a digraph that may have parallel arcs, a source $s\in V$,
terminals $T\subseteq V$, demands $d\in\Q_{\ge0}^T$, and a fractional flow $x\in\Q_{\ge0}^A$ that is feasible: its
divergence is $\sum_t d_t$ at $s$, $-d_t$ at each terminal $t$ and $0$ elsewhere. Throughout this literature the
capacity of an arc is taken to be $x(a)$ itself, so that violation is measured against the flow being rounded
\cite{TVZ24}. Write $\dmax=\max_{t\in T}d_t$. An unsplittable flow, or routing, $\mathcal P=\{P^t\}_{t\in T}$ assigns one
simple $s$--$t$ path to each terminal; its load is
\begin{equation}
\flow^{\mathcal P}(a)\;=\;\sum_{t\in T\colon a\in P^t}d_t .
\label{eq:load}
\end{equation}
A cost vector $c\in\Q_{\ge0}^A$ prices one unit of flow on each arc, so the cost of a load is $c^{\mathsf T}\flow^{\mathcal P}$.
The restriction to simple paths loses nothing: appending a cycle to a walk adds load on arcs and, because $c\ge0$,
adds cost, so a routing on walks is never better in either condition than the routing on simple paths obtained by deleting
the cycles \,[D]. It is also the convention of the sources, and the library enumerates simple paths only
(Section~\ref{sec:enum}). The two conditions on a routing are
\begin{align}
\text{congestion-good:}\quad & \flow^{\mathcal P}(a)\le x(a)+\dmax\ \ \text{for every arc }a\in A,
\label{eq:congestion}\\
\text{cost-good:}\quad & c^{\mathsf T}\flow^{\mathcal P}\le c^{\mathsf T}x .
\label{eq:cost}
\end{align}
Both inequalities are inclusive. Congestion-goodness alone is the theorem of Dinitz, Garg and Goemans \cite{DGG99}, so a
congestion-good routing exists on every instance; cost-goodness alone is achieved by routing every terminal on a cheapest
path (Proposition~\ref{prop:cheapest}). The content of Goemans' conjecture is the simultaneity of the two conditions
(conjecture below). Both references \cite{STVZ25,TVZ24} note that the underlying digraph may be assumed acyclic: cancelling
flow on directed cycles and deleting arcs with zero flow yields an instance that is no easier.

\begin{namedconj}
For every instance and every $c\in\Q^A_{\ge0}$ there is an unsplittable flow that is both congestion-good and cost-good.
\end{namedconj}

\subsection{The violation constants}
\label{sec:constants}

The conjecture fixes the budget of overload at one unit of $\dmax$. To measure how far an instance is from satisfying it,
let the budget of a routing be the least multiple of $\dmax$ at which it becomes congestion-good,
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\mathcal P)\;=\;\max_{a\in A}\frac{\bigl(\flow^{\mathcal P}(a)-x(a)\bigr)^{+}}{\dmax},
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
and define the violation that an instance forces under given costs, and the largest violation that any pricing forces,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)\;=\;\min\bigl\{\alpha(\mathcal P)\colon c^{\mathsf T}\flow^{\mathcal P}\le c^{\mathsf T}x\bigr\},
\qquad
\alpha_{\max}(G,d,x)\;=\;\max_{c\ge0}\ \alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c).
\label{eq:frontier}
\end{equation}
The minimum in the first definition ranges over a finite nonempty set, because the cheapest-path routing is cost-good, and
the maximum in the second is attained because $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)$ takes finitely many values. We call
$\alpha_{\max}$ the frontier value of the instance. The frontier constant is
\begin{equation}
\alpha^\star\;=\;\sup_{(G,s,T,d,x)}\ \alpha_{\max}(G,d,x),
\label{eq:alphastar}
\end{equation}
the least $\alpha$ such that every instance and every cost vector admit a routing with load at most $x+\alpha\dmax$ and cost at
most $c^{\mathsf T}x$; its planar version $\alpha^\star_{\mathrm{planar}}$ takes the supremum over planar instances. Goemans'
conjecture asserts $\alpha^\star\le1$, and an instance is a counterexample for some cost vector exactly when
$\alpha_{\max}>1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:frontier}). The constant normalised by Nikolenko is the same quantity
\cite{Nik1,Nik2,Nik3} [V]. The only upper bound known is $\alpha^\star_{\mathrm{planar}}\le2$ \cite{TVZ24}, and no finite
upper bound is known in general. The lower bounds that this paper checks are $\alpha^\star\ge\tfrac{16}{15}$ for the seven-vertex
instance, $\alpha^\star\ge1.28260069\ldots$ for the 17-terminal refinement and $\alpha^\star_{\mathrm{planar}}\ge
1.17353531974518$ for the planar record (Table~\ref{tab:records}). The value $\tfrac98$ and the value $(299-41\sqrt{41})/32$
are suprema of families, not values of single instances.

\subsection{The lattice of conjectures}
\label{sec:lattice}

Four conjectures about unsplittable flow circulate, with the labels of \cite{STVZ25}. Conjecture~1.2 is Goemans'.
Conjecture~1.3 is the Morell--Skutella statement without costs: on an acyclic digraph some routing satisfies
$x_a-\dmax\le\flow^{\mathcal P}(a)\le x_a+\dmax$ everywhere; it is their Conjecture~1 \cite{MS22}. Conjecture~1.4 adds
$c^{\mathsf T}\flow^{\mathcal P}\le c^{\mathsf T}x$ to the two-sided bounds and is their Conjecture~2. Conjecture~1.5 asks for the
two-sided bounds with $2\dmax$ in place of $\dmax$ together with the cost bound. The implications used below are:
Conjecture~1.4 implies 1.3 (drop the cost clause) and implies 1.2 (drop the lower bounds, and use the reduction to acyclic
instances); and Theorem~1.6 of \cite{STVZ25} states that 1.3 implies 1.5. The numbering 1.3 and 1.4 belongs to \cite{STVZ25};
the paper of Morell and Skutella numbers the same statements 1 and 2 \cite{MS22}. The cost-free statement 1.3 is false in general
\cite{BLRT26}, so the hypothesis of Theorem~1.6 of \cite{STVZ25} is false and that theorem no longer gives a route to
Conjecture~1.5; the status of 1.5 is open in the sources read here. A refutation of 1.2 on an acyclic instance refutes 1.4
as well, and the convex-combination form of \cite{MSS07,MSW25}, in which $x$ is a convex combination of congestion-good
unsplittable flows, implies the cost form by the easy direction: $x=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i$ gives $\min_ic^{\mathsf T}y_i\le c^{\mathsf T}x$.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The procedures below are those of the exact library \texttt{ufclib} of the repository. They were written from the statement of the
conjecture before the lower-bound series appeared, validated on hand-built instances (Section~\ref{sec:design}) and unchanged
for the computations of Section~\ref{sec:results}. Every quantity is a \texttt{fractions.Fraction}; the instance constructor refuses
floating-point input, and a token scan of the library and of the experiment code finds no float literal outside one negative control.
Table~\ref{tab:procedures} gives the cost of each procedure in the size of the instance and the time measured on the records.

\subsection{Instance model and enumeration of paths and routings}
\label{sec:enum}

An instance stores its arcs by index, each with a tail, a head, an exact fractional load $x_a$ and an exact unit cost $c_a$.
Indexing arcs rather than vertex pairs makes parallel arcs distinct paths: a model keyed on pairs of endpoints would find one path
where two exist, and the validation instance with two parallel arcs separates the two models (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). The
constructor checks flow conservation at every vertex and nonnegativity of $x$ and $c$, so an infeasible flow is rejected before any
decision is made. Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum} enumerates the simple $s$--$t$ paths of every terminal by its own depth-first search,
guarding on vertices rather than arcs so that the search terminates on digraphs with directed cycles. The enumeration never takes a
list of paths from the data. Its completeness is the failure mode of candidate counterexamples in this area, and for that reason
every computation of Section~\ref{sec:results} asserts the path count per terminal against an independent count, and uses the
theorem of Dinitz, Garg and Goemans as an oracle: an enumeration that reports no congestion-good routing signals an error in the data
or in the code, never in the theorem.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Simple paths and routings of an instance. The routings are generated in a fixed terminal order and never stored together when
the instance is large; the load vector of a routing is computed on demand.}
\label{alg:enum}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an instance $(G,s,T,d,x,c)$ with exact rational data
\Ensure the list $\Pi_t$ of simple $s$--$t$ paths of each terminal $t$, and the routings $\prod_{t}\Pi_t$
\For{each terminal $t\in T$}
  \State $\Pi_t\gets\emptyset$; \ \textsc{Dfs}$(s,\langle\rangle)$ with the visited set $\{s\}$
  \Comment{\textsc{Dfs}$(v,\pi)$: if $v=t$ append $\pi$ to $\Pi_t$ and return; otherwise for each arc $a=(v,w)$ with $w$ unvisited, mark $w$, call \textsc{Dfs}$(w,\pi\cdot a)$, unmark $w$}
\EndFor
\State assert that $|\Pi_t|$ equals the independently obtained path count of $t$ for every $t$
\State \Return $\Pi_t$ for every $t$, and the generator of all routings $(P^t)_{t\in T}\in\prod_t\Pi_t$, with $\flow^{\mathcal P}$ by equation~\eqref{eq:load}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The number of routings is $\prod_tp_t$, which is $2^{|T|}$ on the instances with two paths per terminal: eight routings for
three terminals and \nkroutings\ for 17. The enumeration has no better worst case, since the number of simple paths of a terminal can be
exponential in the size of the graph; the instances treated here have at most two paths per terminal.

\subsection{Decision of an instance}
\label{sec:decide}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:decide} decides whether an instance, with its given cost vector, is a counterexample. For each routing it computes
the load, the violation of equation~\eqref{eq:congestion} on each arc, the budget of equation~\eqref{eq:alpha} and the cost of
equation~\eqref{eq:cost}. The instance is a counterexample exactly when no routing is both congestion-good and cost-good; the library
also returns the number of congestion-good routings, their least cost and $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=\min\{\alpha(\mathcal P)\colon\mathcal P
\text{ cost-good}\}$. The inequalities are inclusive, in agreement with the statement of the conjecture in \cite{STVZ25}: the validation instance $V_4$ has a routing that loads an arc to exactly
$x_a+\dmax$, and the decision classifies it as congestion-good. For the instances with $\nkroutings$ routings the decision is made by
a streaming loop that uses the library's own routing, load and budget functions and keeps one load vector at a time; the loop agrees with
\texttt{decide\_instance} on every instance of at most 64 routings.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Decision of an instance with given costs: enumeration of all routings, the two goodness conditions and the forced violation.}
\label{alg:decide}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an instance with a feasible $x$ and a cost vector $c\ge0$
\Ensure whether a routing is both congestion-good and cost-good; $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}$; the least cost among congestion-good routings
\State $m^\ast\gets\infty$; \ $\alpha^\ast\gets\infty$; \ $g\gets0$
\For{each routing $\mathcal P$ from Algorithm~\ref{alg:enum}}
  \State $y\gets\flow^{\mathcal P}$; \ $\alpha\gets\max_a(y_a-x_a)^+/\dmax$; \ $\kappa\gets c^{\mathsf T}y$
  \If{$\alpha\le1$} \State $g\gets g+1$; \ $m^\ast\gets\min(m^\ast,\kappa)$ \EndIf
  \If{$\kappa\le c^{\mathsf T}x$} \State $\alpha^\ast\gets\min(\alpha^\ast,\alpha)$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return counterexample $\iff\alpha^\ast>1$ (no routing is both congestion-good and cost-good); \ $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=\alpha^\ast$; \ $m^\ast$; \ $g$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The separation linear program}
\label{sec:sep}

Let $\mathcal U(x)$ be the finite set of load vectors of the congestion-good routings. It does not depend on $c$, and it is nonempty
by the theorem of Dinitz, Garg and Goemans \cite{DGG99}. Condition~\eqref{eq:cost} is invariant under positive scaling of $c$, so the
search over cost vectors can be normalised to $\sum_ac_a=1$ and written as a linear program in the variables $(\delta,c)$,
\begin{equation}
(\mathrm{SEP})\qquad \max\ \delta\quad\text{subject to}\quad c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)\ge\delta\ \ \forall y\in\mathcal U(x),\qquad
\textstyle\sum_ac_a=1,\qquad c\ge0 .
\label{eq:sep}
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:sep} proves that an instance admits a cost vector that makes it a counterexample if and only if the optimum of
\eqref{eq:sep} is positive. Algorithm~\ref{alg:sep} builds the rows from the enumeration and solves the program exactly. The free variable $\delta$
is split into a difference of two nonnegative parts so that the negative optima of instances that obey the conjecture can be returned.
The program has $|\mathcal U(x)|$ constraints and $|A|+2$ variables, and removing the cost vector from the search is the point: an
exhaustive hunt for counterexamples ranges over graphs, demands and fractional flows only, with one exact program per instance.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Separation program: does any nonnegative cost vector make the instance a counterexample, and what is the largest normalised gap?}
\label{alg:sep}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an instance $(G,s,T,d,x)$ with exact data
\Ensure the optimum $\delta^\star$ of equation~\eqref{eq:sep} and a witness $c$
\State rows $\gets\{\,y-x\colon y=\flow^{\mathcal P}\text{ for a congestion-good routing }\mathcal P\,\}$; \textbf{assert} rows $\neq\emptyset$
\State solve $\min\,-(\delta^+-\delta^-)$ subject to $\delta^+-\delta^--c^{\mathsf T}r\le0$ for each row $r$, $\sum_ac_a=1$, $\delta^\pm,c\ge0$ with Algorithm~\ref{alg:bland}
\State \Return $\delta^\star=\delta^+-\delta^-$ and $c$; the instance admits a counterexample cost vector iff $\delta^\star>0$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The frontier value and the exact simplex}
\label{sec:frontier}

The frontier value $\alpha_{\max}$ of equation~\eqref{eq:frontier} is computed from the same program restricted to the routings below a level. For a level
$A$ write $\mathcal U_A=\{\flow^{\mathcal P}\colon\alpha(\mathcal P)<A\}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:frontier} shows that $\alpha_{\max}$ is the largest value $A$
among the distinct budgets $\alpha(\mathcal P)$ for which the program
\begin{equation}
\max\ \delta\quad\text{subject to}\quad c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)\ge\delta\ \ \forall y\in\mathcal U_A,\qquad\textstyle\sum_ac_a=1,\qquad c\ge0
\label{eq:levellp}
\end{equation}
has a positive optimum, and that the budget of a cheapest routing is a ceiling on it. Algorithm~\ref{alg:frontier} evaluates every distinct level in
increasing order. The linear programs are solved by a two-phase simplex written for this work, Algorithm~\ref{alg:bland}, with Bland's rule: the entering
variable is the one of smallest index with a negative reduced cost, and the leaving variable is the one of smallest basis index among the ties of the
ratio test. The rule cannot cycle, so the solver terminates on degenerate programs by construction. It replaced the rational simplex of sympy, which raised
an oscillation error on a degenerate member of the spine family of Section~\ref{sec:family}; the two solvers agree on every instance on which both finish
(Table~\ref{tab:controls}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Frontier value by enumeration: one separation program per distinct budget level, on the routings strictly below the level.}
\label{alg:frontier}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an instance with exact data and at most a few thousand routings
\Ensure $\alpha_{\max}$, a witness cost vector, and the ceiling
\State collect, for every routing, $\bigl(\alpha(\mathcal P),\flow^{\mathcal P},c^{\mathsf T}\flow^{\mathcal P}\bigr)$; sort the distinct budgets $A_1<\dots<A_L$; ceiling $\gets\alpha$ of a routing of least cost
\State $\alpha_{\max}\gets A_1$ \Comment{$\mathcal U_{A_1}=\emptyset$, so the lowest level is positive}
\For{$i=2,\dots,L$}
  \State solve \eqref{eq:levellp} for $A_i$ over the rows $\{\flow^{\mathcal P}\colon\alpha(\mathcal P)<A_i\}$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sep}); \textbf{if} $\delta^\star>0$ \textbf{then} $\alpha_{\max}\gets A_i$, witness $\gets c$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\alpha_{\max}$, witness, ceiling
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Two-phase simplex in exact rational arithmetic with Bland's rule, for $\min c^{\mathsf T}z$ subject to $A_{\mathrm{ub}}z\le b_{\mathrm{ub}}$, $A_{\mathrm{eq}}z=b_{\mathrm{eq}}$, $z\ge0$.}
\label{alg:bland}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require rational matrices and vectors
\Ensure the optimum and an optimal $z$, or a report of infeasibility or unboundedness
\State add slack variables to the inequalities; negate every row with a negative right-hand side so that $b\ge0$
\State \textbf{Phase I:} append one artificial variable per row, minimise their sum by steps 3--5; if the minimum is positive, report infeasibility; otherwise drive the artificials out of the basis
\State \textbf{pivot loop:} $j\gets\min\{\,j\colon r_j<0\,\}$ (smallest index with a negative reduced cost); if none, stop
\State leaving row $\gets$ the row with $a_{ij}>0$ minimising $b_i/a_{ij}$, ties broken by the smallest basic variable index; if no row, report unboundedness
\State pivot on $(i,j)$ over the rationals; return to step 3
\State \textbf{Phase II:} the same loop on the original objective from the feasible basis of Phase I
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The cutting-plane evaluator}
\label{sec:cp}

The enumerative evaluation of Algorithm~\ref{alg:frontier} stores every load vector and solves one dense program over all routings below each level; it does not finish
on instances with tens of thousands of routings. The cutting-plane evaluator, Algorithm~\ref{alg:cp}, never holds $\mathcal U_A$. A first streaming pass over the routings collects the sorted distinct
levels $A_1<\dots<A_L$ and the ceiling, and stores nothing else. Positivity of a level is monotone, because $\mathcal U_A$ grows with $A$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:cp}), so a binary search over the levels needs $O(\log L)$ level tests. A level test is a loop. The separation program is solved over a set of cuts, rows that belong to $\mathcal U_A$,
giving a cost vector $c$; an oracle then returns the routing of least cost $c^{\mathsf T}y$ among those with $\alpha(\mathcal P)<A$. If that routing is strictly dearer than $x$ under $c$, then $c$
separates every routing of $\mathcal U_A$ and the level is positive; otherwise the routing is a violated constraint and becomes a cut. If the program over the cuts has optimum at most $0$ the level is not positive,
because the cuts are a subset of its constraints. The oracle is a branch and bound over the terminals, largest demand first. A branch is cut when a partial load already satisfies
$y_a\ge x_a+A\,\dmax$ on some arc, since loads only grow as terminals are added and no completion can then lie below the level, or when the partial cost plus the cheapest remaining path of every undecided terminal
cannot beat the best routing found. The cuts found at the highest positive level so far are reused at the next level, since they belong to the smaller set $\mathcal U_{A'}\subseteq\mathcal U_A$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Cutting-plane evaluator of the frontier value: streaming levels, binary search over them and a loop of separation programs and an exact oracle at each level.}
\label{alg:cp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require an instance with exact data; \ \textbf{Ensure} $\alpha_{\max}$, witness, ceiling and the list of tested levels
\State one pass over the routings: levels $A_1<\dots<A_L$ (distinct values of $\alpha$), ceiling; $\ell\gets1$, $h\gets L+1$, cuts $\gets\emptyset$ \Comment{$A_\ell$ positive, $A_{h}$ and above not positive}
\While{$h-\ell>1$}
  \State $\mu\gets\lfloor(\ell+h)/2\rfloor$; \ run \textsc{LevelPositive}$(A_\mu,\text{cuts})$; \textbf{if} positive \textbf{then} $\ell\gets\mu$, keep witness and cuts \textbf{else} $h\gets\mu$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $A_\ell$, witness, ceiling, tested levels
\Statex
\Function{LevelPositive}{$A$, cuts}
  \Loop
    \State $(\delta,c)\gets$ Algorithm~\ref{alg:sep} over the rows cuts; \textbf{if} $\delta\le0$ \textbf{return} not positive
    \State $(y,\kappa)\gets$ oracle: the routing of least $c^{\mathsf T}y$ with $\alpha<A$ (branch and bound); \textbf{if} none, \textbf{return} positive
    \State \textbf{if} $\kappa>c^{\mathsf T}x$ \textbf{return} positive; \ \textbf{else} append $y-x$ to cuts
  \EndLoop
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Planarity certificates and their checker}
\label{sec:planar}

Planarity is decided on the simple undirected graph of the arcs, with parallel arcs merged and loops dropped, which does not change planarity.
A solver (the left-right planarity test of Brandes \cite{Bra09} as implemented in \texttt{networkx} \nnetworkx) answers, and the answer is accepted only through a certificate that a separate checker verifies; the checker never
calls the solver. For a planar answer the certificate is a rotation system, the clockwise order of the neighbours at each vertex. The checker traces the faces by the rule that
from the dart $(u,v)$ the next dart is $(v,w)$ with $w$ the predecessor of $u$ in the clockwise rotation at $v$, counts the faces $F$, and accepts when $V-E+F=2$ on every connected component. For a
non-planar answer the certificate is a Kuratowski subgraph; the checker verifies that it is a subgraph of the instance graph, suppresses its vertices of degree two, and accepts when the reduced graph
is $K_5$ (five vertices, all of degree four) or $K_{3,3}$ (six vertices, all of degree three, bipartite). For the smallest instances a cheaper test settles planarity first: a graph with fewer than
five vertices of degree at least four and fewer than six of degree at least three has no subdivision of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$, since the branch vertices of such a subdivision have those degrees, and is
planar by Kuratowski's theorem. The test returns no answer when it cannot decide, and is recorded next to the certificate.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Certified planarity: a solver proposes a rotation system or a Kuratowski subgraph, and an independent checker accepts only a valid certificate.}
\label{alg:planar}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the arcs of an instance
\Ensure planar or non-planar, with an accepted certificate, or rejection
\State $\Gamma\gets$ simple undirected graph of the arcs; ask the solver for $(\text{planar?},\text{certificate})$
\If{planar} \State trace the faces of the rotation system; compute $V-E+F$ on each component; \textbf{accept} iff it equals $2$ on all components, and the rotation at each vertex is exactly the neighbour set
\Else \State check that every edge of the subgraph is an edge of $\Gamma$; suppress degree-two vertices; \textbf{accept} iff the reduced graph is $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$
\EndIf
\State \Return the answer and the certificate that was accepted; a rejected certificate is reported and no planarity statement is made
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

This section proves the statements on which the procedures of Section~\ref{sec:methods} and the readings of Section~\ref{sec:results} rest. Each is a
finite argument; the instances of the experiments are used only to illustrate them. All are marked [D].

\subsection{Cheapest-path routings and the single-terminal theorem}
\label{sec:theory-single}

\begin{proposition}[cheapest paths are cost-good]
\label{prop:cheapest}
For every instance and every $c\ge0$, the routing that sends each terminal $t$ along a path of least cost is cost-good. Consequently the set in the
definition of $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)$ is nonempty and $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)\le\alpha(\mathcal R_c)$ for every cheapest-path routing $\mathcal R_c$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Decompose the feasible flow $x$ into $s$--$t$ paths and directed cycles. Every path starts at $s$ and ends at a terminal, and the weights of the paths that end at
$t$ add up to $d_t$. Let $\gamma_t$ be the least cost of a unit of flow on an $s$--$t$ path. The cycles have nonnegative cost, so
\[
c^{\mathsf T}x\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in T}\ \sum_{P\text{ ending at }t}w_P\,c(P)\;\ge\;\sum_{t\in T}d_t\gamma_t ,
\]
and the right-hand side is the cost of the routing in which each terminal uses a cheapest path.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[one terminal]
\label{thm:single}
No instance with a single terminal is a counterexample, for any nonnegative cost vector.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
With $T=\{t\}$ the maximum demand is $d_t$. A routing loads its path with $d_t$ and every other arc with $0$, and $d_t\le x_a+d_t$ holds because $x_a\ge0$, so every
routing is congestion-good. By Proposition~\ref{prop:cheapest} the cheapest-path routing is cost-good, hence it is both.
\end{proof}

The statement is the first rung of any minimality question: a counterexample needs at least two terminals. The next subsection shows what the argument gives at two.

\subsection{A necessary condition at two terminals}
\label{sec:theory-two}

\begin{proposition}[two terminals]
\label{prop:two}
Let $T=\{1,2\}$ and let $R_1,R_2$ be cheapest paths. The cheapest-path routing is cost-good. It is congestion-good if and only if every arc lying on both paths
carries $x_a\ge\min(d_1,d_2)$. Consequently any two-terminal counterexample contains an arc on both cheapest paths, for every choice of cheapest paths, with
$x_a<\min(d_1,d_2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first statement is Proposition~\ref{prop:cheapest}. An arc on exactly one of the two paths carries $d_i\le\dmax\le x_a+\dmax$. An arc on both carries $d_1+d_2$, and
$d_1+d_2\le x_a+\max(d_1,d_2)$ holds exactly when $x_a\ge\min(d_1,d_2)$. The last statement is the contrapositive: if the condition held for some pair of cheapest paths, that
routing would be congestion-good and cost-good.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
A natural argument for two terminals is invalid. One might conclude that no counterexample exists with at most two terminals because a conflict graph on at most two nodes
has no odd cycle. A terminal may have many path choices, so the conflict graph need not have two nodes, and the step from an integral stable-set polytope to the absence of a separating
nonnegative cost vector is unproved. The two-terminal case is open [C]; Proposition~\ref{prop:two} locates where a counterexample would have to lie.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Correctness of the separation program}
\label{sec:theory-sep}

\begin{proposition}[separation]
\label{prop:sep}
Let $\mathcal U(x)$ be the finite nonempty set of loads of congestion-good routings. The program~\eqref{eq:sep} is feasible and bounded, and its optimum $\delta^\star$ is positive
if and only if there is a cost vector $c\ge0$ for which the instance is a counterexample.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The program is feasible: any $c\ge0$ with $\sum_ac_a=1$ gives some $\delta$, possibly negative. It is bounded: $\delta\le c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)\le\max_a|y_a-x_a|$ for any fixed $y\in\mathcal U(x)$.
If $\delta^\star>0$ with a witness $c$, then every congestion-good routing satisfies $c^{\mathsf T}y\ge c^{\mathsf T}x+\delta^\star>c^{\mathsf T}x$, so none is cost-good and the instance is a counterexample for $c$.
Conversely let $c\ge0$, $c\ne0$, make the instance a counterexample. Then $\min_{y\in\mathcal U(x)}c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)>0$, because the minimum is over a finite nonempty set of strictly positive numbers. Rescaling $c$ by
$1/\sum_ac_a>0$ preserves that inequality and gives a feasible point of~\eqref{eq:sep} with a positive objective.
\end{proof}

Because $\mathcal U(x)$ does not depend on $c$, the reduction removes the cost vector from the search space. A reduction of this kind was sketched, without proof, in the material that accompanied the announcement; the proof above is
written here, and the implementation is exact (Section~\ref{sec:sep}).

\subsection{The frontier value and the validity of the level search}
\label{sec:theory-frontier}

\begin{proposition}[frontier value]
\label{prop:frontier}
Let $A_1<\dots<A_L$ be the distinct values of $\alpha(\mathcal P)$ over all routings. For a level $A$ let $\mathcal U_A$ be the loads of routings with $\alpha(\mathcal P)<A$. Then
(i) there is a $c\ge0$ with $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)\ge A$ if and only if the program~\eqref{eq:levellp} has a positive optimum, with the convention that an empty $\mathcal U_A$ is positive;
(ii) $\alpha_{\max}=\max\{A_i\colon\eqref{eq:levellp}\text{ is positive at }A_i\}$;
(iii) $\alpha_{\max}>1$ if and only if the instance is a counterexample for some $c\ge0$, and the program~\eqref{eq:sep} is the program~\eqref{eq:levellp} at the least level $A_i>1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)\ge A$ holds exactly when no routing with $\alpha(\mathcal P)<A$ is cost-good, that is, when $c^{\mathsf T}y>c^{\mathsf T}x$ for every $y\in\mathcal U_A$. The scaling argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:sep}, with $\mathcal U_A$ in place of $\mathcal U(x)$, turns this into the positivity of~\eqref{eq:levellp}. (ii) The values of $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)$ are among the $A_i$, so the maximum over $c$ is the
largest $A_i$ that some $c$ reaches, and (i) tests exactly that. At $A_1$ the set $\mathcal U_{A_1}$ is empty, so the lowest level is always reached. (iii) By (ii), $\alpha_{\max}>1$ iff some $c$ has $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}(c)>1$, that is, iff
no routing is both congestion-good ($\alpha\le1$) and cost-good for that $c$; congestion-good routings are those with $\alpha(\mathcal P)\le1$, which is $\mathcal U_A$ for the least level $A>1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[the level search]
\label{prop:cp}
(i) If a level $A'$ is positive and $A\le A'$ then $A$ is positive, so positivity is monotone along the sorted levels and a binary search finds the largest positive level.
(ii) Cuts that belong to $\mathcal U_{A'}$ belong to $\mathcal U_A$ for $A'\le A$, so the cuts of the highest positive level may be reused. (iii) If the program over a set of cuts has optimum $\le0$, the level is not positive.
(iv) If the program over the cuts has optimum $\delta>0$ with solution $c$, and the cheapest routing $y$ of $\mathcal U_A$ under $c$ satisfies $c^{\mathsf T}y>c^{\mathsf T}x$, the level is positive; otherwise $y$ is a new cut, and the loop terminates.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) and (ii) follow from $\mathcal U_A\subseteq\mathcal U_{A'}$ for $A\le A'$, since a vector $c$ that makes every element of the larger set dearer than $x$ does so for the smaller set. (iii) The program over a subset of the constraints has an optimum at
least as large as the full program, so a nonpositive value bounds the full optimum from above. (iv) In the first case $c$ satisfies every constraint of~\eqref{eq:levellp} with $\delta'=\min_{y\in\mathcal U_A}c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)>0$, so the optimum
is positive. In the second case $c^{\mathsf T}(y-x)\le0<\delta$, while every existing cut satisfies $c^{\mathsf T}(r)\ge\delta>0$, so $y$ is not among the cuts. The number of cuts is bounded by $|\mathcal U_A|$, which is finite, so after finitely many
rounds one of the stopping cases occurs.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Why a certificate decides planarity}
\label{sec:theory-planar}

\begin{proposition}[certificates]
\label{prop:planar}
(i) A rotation system whose faces satisfy $V-E+F=2$ on every connected component defines an embedding of the graph in the sphere, so the graph is planar.
(ii) A subgraph that becomes $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$ after the suppression of its vertices of degree two shows that the graph is not planar.
(iii) A graph with fewer than five vertices of degree at least four and fewer than six vertices of degree at least three is planar.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) A rotation system defines a cellular embedding of each connected component in a closed orientable surface; face tracing lists its faces, and the Euler characteristic of the surface satisfies $V-E+F=2-2g$ with $g$ the genus.
A value of $2$ forces $g=0$, the sphere. (ii) A subdivision of a graph is planar if and only if the graph is, and a subgraph of a planar graph is planar. Hence it suffices that $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ are not planar. A simple planar graph with $V\ge3$ has
$E\le3V-6$; $K_5$ has $V=5$ and $E=10>9$. A triangle-free simple planar graph has every face of length at least four, so $2E\ge4F$, and Euler's formula $V-E+F=2$ gives $E\le2V-4$; $K_{3,3}$ is bipartite with $V=6$ and
$E=9>8$. (iii) By Kuratowski's theorem a nonplanar graph contains a subdivision of $K_5$, whose five branch vertices have degree at least four, or of $K_{3,3}$, whose six branch vertices have degree at least three. Neither
fits the hypothesis.
\end{proof}

The seven-vertex instance has no vertex of degree four or more and four vertices of degree three, so (iii) shows that it is planar without an embedding computation; the larger instances use the certificates of (i) and (ii).

\subsection{The conflict bound}
\label{sec:theory-conflict}

Let each terminal $i$ have a free path $Z_i$ of cost $0$ and a paid path $E_i$ of cost $\pi_i$ when carrying its demand, and no other path. Call two free choices $Z_i,Z_j$ \emph{in conflict} when every choice of the paths of the remaining terminals
violates the congestion bound on some arc. A conflict can be mediated: a terminal that is not party to either choice may load an arc on both of its paths. Let $H$ be the conflict graph on the free choices, with independence number $\mathrm{ind}(H)$.

\begin{proposition}[conflict bound]
\label{prop:conflict}
Every congestion-good routing selects a set of free choices that is independent in $H$, so it uses at least $|T|-\mathrm{ind}(H)$ paid choices and costs at least the sum of the $|T|-\mathrm{ind}(H)$ smallest $\pi_i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If a congestion-good routing selected two free choices in conflict, the paths of the remaining terminals would be a completion of that pair, and by the definition of conflict it would violate the bound on some arc,
a contradiction. So the selected free choices are pairwise non-conflicting, that is, independent in $H$, and the other terminals use their paid paths, each of positive cost $\pi_i$.
\end{proof}

For three terminals with a triangle $H$ the bound gives $\pi_i+\pi_j$ for the two cheapest paid choices. With $\rho_i=x(Z_i)/d_i$, the fractional flow buys $\sum_i\rho_i$ units of free routing, while an independent set
buys at most $\mathrm{ind}(H)$: when $\sum_i\rho_i>\mathrm{ind}(H)$ the fractional flow violates a stable-set inequality of $H$, and costs that make the paid paths dear separate it. Section~\ref{sec:seven} evaluates this
on the seven-vertex instance, where the bound reaches the minimum cost of a congestion-good routing exactly.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

Seven archived computations, \EXP{001} to \EXP{007}, produced the results of Section~\ref{sec:results}. Each states, before its code was run, the predictions that
the computation can refute. Pre-registration matters for the inference here because several predictions concern values that a reader could adjust to the output, such as the frontier
value of the seven-vertex instance, and because predictions can fail: the search of \EXP{004} for a larger forced violation did, and is reported as a null result. All computations are deterministic, use exact rational arithmetic, run on a CPU without randomness
(the one random draw, in stage C of the odd-cycle sweep, has a recorded seed), and write their full output to the archive.

\paragraph{\EXP{001}: calibration of the decision procedure.} The exact checker is calibrated on five instances whose answers are fixed by inspection before the run. $V_1$ has one terminal
with $d=2$, a direct arc of cost $5$ and a free detour; $V_2$ has two parallel arcs $s\to t$ with costs $0$ and $7$; $V_3$ has fractional loads $\tfrac12$; $V_4$ has two terminals and a routing that loads an
arc to exactly $x_a+\dmax$; and $V_5$ is $V_1$ with a directed cycle on two further vertices. The predictions are the hand-counted number of simple paths per terminal (two, two, two, two and one, and three), the number of routings as the
product of the path counts, the rejection of a corrupted copy of $V_1$ whose flow violates conservation, the existence of a congestion-good routing on every instance, the known minimum cost, the inclusive boundary on $V_4$,
termination and acyclicity detection on $V_5$, and the absence of floating-point values.

\paragraph{\EXP{002}: the seven-vertex instance.} The instance is re-entered by hand from the published table rather than parsed from the proposer's file, so that a transcription error in either direction is visible, and
the proposer's verifier is archived with its SHA-256 digest and is never imported or executed. The predictions are the feasibility of $x$, exactly two simple paths per terminal, $c^{\mathsf T}x=58$, exactly four congestion-good
routings with minimum cost $60$, the forced violation $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=\tfrac{16}{15}$, the tests of the consistency battery of Table~\ref{tab:battery}, the conflict triangle with its fractional selection point, and, after the decision is made, agreement with
the numbers reported publicly. A prediction was stated to fail the claim if a congestion-good and cost-good routing existed, or if the enumeration found a path that the certificate did not consider.

\paragraph{\EXP{003}: the separation program.} The derivation of Section~\ref{sec:theory-sep} was written before the run. The predictions are that the optimum on the seven-vertex instance is at least $\tfrac27$ (the published cost vector has coordinate sum $7$ and gap $2$), that the optimum is at most $0$ on the five calibration instances, that the witness fed back
to the decision procedure reproduces the decision that the instance is a counterexample, that no single-terminal instance of a set built to look adversarial is a counterexample, and that the characterisation of Proposition~\ref{prop:two} holds on every two-terminal instance tested. The two-terminal family has one shared spine arc and two choices per terminal,
demands $(2,3)$, $(3,5)$, $(4,5)$ and $(5,7)$, every integer split of each demand between its two choices and four cost pairs, \ntwoterm\ instances in all. The sweep is evidence over a bounded box, and its scope is fixed in advance as such.

\paragraph{\EXP{004}: the frontier value and the spine family.} The family has a zero-cost spine $s=v_0\to v_1\to\dots\to v_m$. Terminal $i$ has demand $d_i$, a free exit at $v_{e_i}$ carrying the fraction $\rho_i$ of its demand at
no cost, and a paid exit at $v_{f_i}$ with $f_i<e_i$, carrying $1-\rho_i$ at the unit cost $c_i$. Spine arc $r$ carries
\begin{equation}
x(v_{r-1},v_r)\;=\;\sum_{i\colon e_i\ge r}\rho_id_i\;+\;\sum_{i\colon f_i\ge r}(1-\rho_i)d_i .
\label{eq:spine}
\end{equation}
The seven-vertex instance is the member with $m=3$, $k=3$, $d=(15,10,15)$, $e=(2,3,3)$, $f=(0,0,1)$, $\rho=(\tfrac13,\tfrac25,\tfrac13)$ and costs $(2,3,2)$; the mediated conflict of its first spine arc comes from $f_3=1$. The predictions are that
the family reproduces the instance arc for arc, that $\alpha_{\max}\le1$ on the five calibration instances, that $\alpha_{\max}=\tfrac{16}{15}$ on the seven-vertex instance (with the alternative $\tfrac{26}{15}$, the budget of the all-free routing, stated as the more interesting outcome), that some family member in a bounded integer box with $k\le4$
terminals and $m\le4$ has a frontier value above $\tfrac{16}{15}$ (low to moderate confidence), and that no member reaches $2$. Two exact simplex implementations are cross-checked on the instances on which the second one finishes.

\paragraph{\EXP{005}: the symmetric three-terminal family.} On the topology of the seven-vertex instance, with demands $(d_1,d_2,d_3)=(1,b,d_3)$, fractions $(r_1,q,r_3)$ of each demand on its free path and costs $1/d_1$, $1/b$ and $1/d_3$ per unit on the three
paid arcs, so that each paid path has integral cost $1$, the note of \cite{Muf26} claims that $b=\tfrac34$, $r_1=r_3=\tfrac14$ and $q=\tfrac12+\varepsilon$ force $\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$. The predictions are that at $\varepsilon\in\{\tfrac1{10},\tfrac1{100},\tfrac1{1000}\}$
the fractional cost is $2-\varepsilon$ and the least violation over the routings that cost no more equals the claimed value; that each member is acyclic, has a $K_4$ subdivision and is planar; that the frontier value is at least the claimed value; and, as an exploration without a predicted value,
the largest frontier value over the grid $d_1=1$, $b\in\{\tfrac18,\dots,\tfrac88\}$, $d_3\in\{\tfrac14,\tfrac24,\tfrac34,\tfrac44\}$, $r_1,q,r_3\in\{\tfrac1{16},\dots,\tfrac{15}{16}\}$, which has $8\cdot4\cdot15^3=\ngridpoints$ points.

\paragraph{\EXP{006}: the odd-cycle family on $C_5$.} Afrasyab's construction \cite{Afr26} realises an odd cycle by acyclic single-source gadgets, one per vertex of the cycle, each with five parameters that the library
names $b$, $\delta$, $r$, $u$ and \emph{approach}; the realisation conditions of the construction are not imposed on the members, and a member is valid when the builder accepts it: acyclic, single source, nonnegative flows with exact
conservation, and three simple paths per terminal. The frontier value is invariant under scaling every demand and flow by one factor, so members are normalised to $\dmax=1$. Stage A is the calibration on the uniform family at
$\varepsilon\in\{\tfrac1{120},\tfrac1{60},\tfrac1{24},\tfrac1{16},\tfrac1{13}\}$, where \cite{Afr26} gives the threshold $\tfrac23-\varepsilon$. Stage B takes the symmetric members, with every gadget equal, with $(b,\delta)\in\{(\tfrac13,1),(\tfrac23,1),(1,1),(1,\tfrac13),(1,\tfrac23)\}$ and $r,u$, approach in $\{0,\tfrac16,\dots,1\}$. Stage C draws random members,
each gadget's five parameters independently ($b,\delta\in\{\tfrac16,\dots,1\}$; $r,u$, approach in $\{0,\tfrac1{12},\dots,1\}$), with seed $20261114$ and redrawing of invalid draws. Stage D is a local search from the ten best members of B and C, in steps of $\pm\tfrac1{24}$ on one parameter of one gadget, accepting strict improvements, with at most 150 evaluations per start.
The predictions are that stage A equals $\tfrac23-\varepsilon$, that no member of B, C or D exceeds $\tfrac98$, and that no member exceeds $1$.

\paragraph{\EXP{007}: the record instances.} The instances are read from a file of 13 entries, 11 concrete and two parametric, transcribed from the three preprints \cite{Nik1,Nik2,Nik3} and four certificate files of the repository \cite{NikRepo}, each entry with its page, theorem or file; the author's code was read as text and
never run or imported. The file has the SHA-256 digest \ninstdigest\ and \ninstbytes\ bytes. The predictions are: (R1) for each of the 11 concrete entries the flow is feasible, every terminal has exactly the paths the source describes, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}$ with the authors' prices equals the claimed value exactly;
(R2) on the four-interval family at $t=\tfrac3{20}$, $\varepsilon=1/n$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=\tfrac{182359}{160000}-\tfrac1n$ for six values of $n$, on the grid $t=k/400$, $40\le k\le80$, at $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ the closed form of Lemma~4.6 of \cite{Nik1} holds, and on the ternary core
$\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}=(3-\eta)^2/8$; (R3) the two 17-terminal instances are non-planar and the four-interval, planar-record and seven-vertex instances are planar, each with a certificate checked by separate code, and the planarity of the triangle template and the ternary core, which the papers do not state, is determined without
a prediction; (R4) the frontier value of each instance is computed, and where it equals the claimed value the author's pricing is optimal on that instance. If the frontier value exceeded a claim, the witness would be re-checked by plain enumeration and the planarity certificate attached before any statement.
Three controls are planned: a change of one unit in the last printed digit of a demand of the 17-terminal record, costs set to zero, and planarity certificates of other graphs or tampered certificates.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.} The measured quantities are exact rationals: budgets $\alpha$, costs, the optimum $\delta^\star$ and the frontier value. There are no repetitions and no seeds except stage C, so the results carry no sampling
variance. Where a statistical vocabulary would apply, the correct statement is that each value is the output of a deterministic finite computation, so it has no confidence interval and no standard deviation; the only intervals in the results are the certified brackets of the level searches and the exact
rational enclosure of an irrational constant (Section~\ref{sec:records}).

\subsection{The seven-vertex instance}
\label{sec:seven}

The instance has the vertices $\{s,u,v,w,t_1,t_2,t_3\}$, demands $d=(15,10,15)$ so that $\dmax=15$, and nine arcs with the fractional loads and the unit costs of Table~\ref{tab:arcs7}, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:instance}.
The flow is feasible, with conservation verified at all seven vertices, and $c^{\mathsf T}x=2\cdot10+3\cdot6+2\cdot10=\nfraccost$ [MV]. The decision procedure finds exactly two simple paths for each terminal: an expensive direct choice
$E_1=st_1$, $E_2=st_2$, $E_3=s\,u\,t_3$ and a free detour $Z_1=s\,u\,v\,t_1$, $Z_2=s\,u\,v\,w\,t_2$, $Z_3=s\,u\,v\,w\,t_3$. Each $E_i$ costs exactly 30 when it carries its demand ($15\cdot2$, $10\cdot3$ and $15\cdot2$) and each $Z_i$ costs zero, so there are
\nroutings\ routings, listed in Table~\ref{tab:routings}.

\paragraph{Consistency with every proved result.} A counterexample must contradict no theorem. Table~\ref{tab:battery} lists each proved result, the test run on the instance and the outcome. All tests pass: the instance has \ngood\ congestion-good routings, so the theorem of
Dinitz, Garg and Goemans is untouched; \ntwowitness\ routings satisfy the cost-free two-sided bounds; the planar theorem is consistent, since a cost-good routing exists with budget $\tfrac{16}{15}\le2$; the demands $\{10,15\}$ are not multiples of one another; the underlying graph is not series-parallel; and the longest $s$--$t$ path has \nmaxpathlen\ arcs, so
the instance is not a two-layer network.

\paragraph{Consequences.} The digraph is acyclic (checked), so any routing satisfying Conjecture~1.4 would satisfy the conclusion of Goemans' conjecture, and Conjecture~1.4 is false as well. If $x$ were a convex combination of congestion-good unsplittable loads $y_i$, then $\min_ic^{\mathsf T}y_i\le c^{\mathsf T}x=58$ would give a congestion-good routing of cost at most $58$, so the convex-combination form is false \cite{MSS07,MSW25}. Conjecture~1.3
survives this instance, but it is false by the cost-free instance of \cite{BLRT26}, which is independent of the one studied here. Conjecture~1.5 is untouched by this instance [D].

\paragraph{The class boundary and planarity.} The branch vertices $\{s,u,v,w\}$ carry six internally disjoint connecting paths, so the underlying graph contains a subdivision of $K_4$ [MV]. Series-parallel digraphs have $K_4$-minor-free underlying graphs [D], and the conjecture is proved for them \cite{MSW25}; this instance lies outside that class by the smallest margin, that is, at the first structure past it. The graph has no vertex of degree four or more and four vertices of degree three, so by
Proposition~\ref{prop:planar}\,(iii) it is planar; the certificate of Section~\ref{sec:records} confirms this with a rotation system. Hence $1<\alpha^\star_{\mathrm{planar}}\le2$ follows from this instance and \cite{TVZ24}; the bounds of Section~\ref{sec:records} supersede the lower end.

\subsection{The separation optimum and the published prices}
\label{sec:sepresult}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:sep} on the seven-vertex instance finds \nsepgood\ congestion-good routings and the optimum $\delta^\star=\nsepopt$ with the witness
\[
c\;=\;\bigl(\tfrac27,\tfrac37,0,\tfrac27,0,0,0,0,0\bigr),
\]
which is exactly the published cost vector $(2,3,0,2,0,0,0,0,0)$ divided by its coordinate sum $7$ [MV]. The optimum is the largest normalised gap that any nonnegative cost vector can force on this graph and this fractional flow. The published prices are therefore an optimal separator for that graph and flow: the 58-versus-60 separation is the best that instance can do, and no reweighting of the arcs
improves it. Feeding the witness back to the decision procedure gives $c^{\mathsf T}x=\tfrac{58}{7}$, minimum congestion-good cost $\tfrac{60}{7}$ and a counterexample, with a gap of $\tfrac27$ that equals the optimum, so the linear program and the enumeration agree. On the \nvalsets\ calibration instances the optimum is exactly $0$ in every case,
so no cost vector turns any of them into a counterexample; this is stronger than the check on their own prices. On the \nsingle\ single-terminal instances (demands 1, 3 and 7, each with two cost levels) every routing is congestion-good and the optimum is at most $0$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:single} requires. The \ntwoterm\ two-terminal instances
of the family of Section~\ref{sec:design} satisfy the characterisation of Proposition~\ref{prop:two} and none admits any cost vector that makes it a counterexample. That is evidence over a bounded box, not a proof; the two-terminal case remains open.

\subsection{The frontier value and the spine family}
\label{sec:family}

The frontier value of the seven-vertex instance is $\alpha_{\max}=\nalphainst$, with the witness above, which is therefore optimal in two senses: it maximises the cost gap (Section~\ref{sec:sepresult}) and the violation forced. The budgets of the eight routings are $\{\tfrac13,\tfrac25,\tfrac23,\tfrac23,\tfrac{16}{15},\tfrac{16}{15},\tfrac{16}{15},\tfrac{26}{15}\}$. The larger value $\nallfreealpha$ of the all-free routing is not attainable by any pricing,
because no nonnegative cost vector makes all seven routings of lower budget simultaneously dearer than $x$ [MV]. The cross-check of two independent exact simplex implementations, ours and that of sympy, agrees on \nxcheck\ instances (the seven-vertex instance, with optimum $\tfrac27$ from both, and the five calibration instances, with optimum $0$).

Over \nfamthree\ parameter points at three terminals and three spine arcs, one is a counterexample, and it is the seven-vertex instance at exactly its published parameters; over \nfamfour\ points at four terminals and four spine arcs, none is. \subsection{The symmetric three-terminal family}
\label{sec:muffatti}

Table~\ref{tab:muffatti} gives the family of \cite{Muf26} at three values of $\varepsilon$. At each, the fractional cost is $2-\varepsilon$ and the least violation over all routings that cost no more equals $\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$ exactly: $\nmufaval$, $\nmufbval$ and $\nmufcval$ at $\varepsilon=\nmufaeps$, $\nmufbeps$ and $\nmufceps$ [MV]. For the two 17-terminal instances, Algorithm~\ref{alg:cp} finishes: the record has \nkreclevels\ distinct levels and \nkrectested\ tested levels,

For the record, the largest positive level is the claimed value \nkreclow\ and the next level above it, \nkrecup, is not positive, a bracket of width $\nkrecgap$; for the refinement the bracket has width $\nkrefgap$, and the witness cost vector of each instance, re-checked by plain enumeration over \nkroutings\ routings, reproduces the frontier value.
The frontier value is therefore not above the claim on any record instance, and a new lower bound cannot come from re-pricing the known instances.

\section{Validation by independent routes}
\label{sec:validation}

Each result of Section~\ref{sec:results} is known to be correct by at least two routes that share no code or no arithmetic, or by a comparison with a published value.

\paragraph{The seven-vertex decision.} The refutation is reached by the complete enumeration of eight routings (Table~\ref{tab:routings}) and, separately, by the conflict bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:conflict}, computed by code that never costs a routing: both give $60>58$.
The enumeration is itself checked by the theorem of Dinitz, Garg and Goemans used as an oracle (a congestion-good routing exists, and \ngood\ do), by the conservation check on $x$, and by the path count of two per terminal against a count made by hand. The formal entry \cite{AFP26} proves the same inequality, $58$ against $60$ at load $x+15$, in a proof assistant for the same nine arcs [V]; it does not formalise the forced violation, the separation optimum or the acyclicity corollaries, which rest on the computations here.

\paragraph{The linear programs.} The separation program and the enumeration agree on the seven-vertex instance in decision and in value: under the witness costs the decision procedure returns $c^{\mathsf T}x=\tfrac{58}{7}$, a least congestion-good cost $\tfrac{60}{7}$ and the gap $\tfrac27$ that equals the optimum. The exact simplex with Bland's rule and the rational simplex of sympy, which share no code, agree exactly on the \nxcheck\ instances on which sympy finishes.
On every instance of the records the witness cost vector returned by the frontier evaluators is re-checked by pricing the instance with it and running the decision procedure, whose enumeration shares nothing with the linear program; the resulting $\alpha_{\mathrm{inst}}$ equals the frontier value in all eleven cases (Table~\ref{tab:records}). For the two 17-terminal instances the check enumerates all \nkroutings\ routings.

\paragraph{The record values.} The streaming loop that decides the 17-terminal instances uses the library's own routing, load and budget functions and is compared with \texttt{decide\_instance} on every instance of at most 64 routings, where they agree; each value equals the printed value to the last digit, and the controls of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} show that a change of $10^{-9}$ in one demand is detected. The values of the seven-vertex instance and of the symmetric family are consistent with the closed
forms of \cite{Muf26} and with the supremum of \cite{Nik1}.

\paragraph{The certificates.} A planarity answer is accepted only through a certificate that code which never calls the solver verifies. The drawings of Figure~\ref{fig:draw} and Figure~\ref{fig:instance} give a third, geometric check by exact segment-intersection tests. The planar solver, the rotation-system checker and the Kuratowski checker are three implementations on one question. All \nctrlc\ certificates of other graphs or tampered certificates that were offered are rejected.

\paragraph{The tests.} The library carries \ntests\ test functions that cover the calibration instances, the separation program, the frontier value, the cutting-plane evaluator and its agreement with the enumerative evaluator on the instances on which both finish, and the odd-cycle builder against the uniform instance of \cite{Afr26} [MV].

\section{Ablation and negative controls}
\label{sec:ablation}

There is no tunable method to ablate here, since every procedure is exact. The ablations are of two kinds: the removal of an ingredient of a procedure, to see what it protects against, and the perturbation of an input, to see that the procedure notices. Table~\ref{tab:controls} lists what was perturbed and what fired.

\input{tab_controls.tex}

Removing the arc index from the instance model would be silent on all of the records except one calibration instance: with two parallel arcs a model keyed on pairs of endpoints finds one path and reports one routing, where the arc-indexed model finds two. A path enumerator that guards on arcs rather than vertices does not terminate on the instance with a directed cycle, and the vertex guard terminates and returns the third simple path
that the cycle creates. An exclusive reading of the congestion inequality would misclassify the routing of the fourth calibration instance that loads an arc to exactly $x_a+\dmax$, and would have silently manufactured counterexamples. Each of these is a plausible implementation error, and each is caught by one calibration instance.

Removing the exact simplex in favour of a single solver would leave the numbers resting on one implementation: sympy's rational simplex raised an oscillation error on a degenerate member of the spine family, and replacing it by the Bland-rule simplex, validated against sympy where both finish, removed the single point of failure. Removing the cuts' reuse or the monotone binary search from the cutting-plane evaluator would leave the enumerative evaluator,
which stores every load vector and one dense program per level, and which is used for the instances of at most 64 routings.

The perturbation of the inputs fired every time. The corrupted flow is rejected by the conservation check. A demand of the 17-terminal record changed in its last printed digit gives a value that no longer equals the claim, for the change carried on the paid path and for the change carried on the free path. Costs set to zero make every routing cost-good, and the value falls to the least budget over all routings, far below the claim on the three instances tried.
The planarity checker rejects the rotation system of the planar record offered for another graph, a rotation with one edge replaced, a rotation with two neighbours swapped (which has genus above $0$) and a Kuratowski subgraph of a graph offered for a different graph. One control is stated as a fact and not as a control: the Kuratowski subgraph of the 17-terminal record is accepted for the refinement, because the two share their graph.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the checks establish.} The seven-vertex instance is a counterexample to the conjecture. This is a finite arithmetic fact, established independently of the announcement, of its authors' code and of any reputation, and the origin of the instance has no bearing on it: the announcement credits a large language model with the discovery \cite{Ryb26}. The instance forces exactly $\tfrac{16}{15}$, which is a lower bound $\alpha^\star\ge\tfrac{16}{15}$ and no more.
The eleven record instances force the values their authors state, are planar or not as stated, and are optimally priced. The checks decide instances; they do not re-prove the suprema, the class results, the mixture characterisation of \cite{Nik3} or the formula for the four-interval family beyond the instances and the grid computed.

\paragraph{Pricing optimality.} The frontier value equals the claimed value on all eleven instances. For an author's instance this means that the prices were found by search and are the best that the instance admits: no re-pricing yields a larger forced violation. Any new lower bound must therefore come from a new instance or a new family, as the open direction of \cite{Nik3} also says, and the evaluators of this paper
give an exact test of a candidate: its frontier value is one number, with a witness. On the seven-vertex instance the same statement holds for the cost gap, at $\tfrac27$.

\paragraph{The extra arc of the planar record.} The topology certificate of the repository for the planar record lists an arc $v_8\to v_9$ with zero flow that the printed instance lacks. An arc without flow is not part of the instance, and removing it leaves the rotation system on the 20 edges of the instance at genus 0, so the planarity claim is unaffected by the discrepancy, which is a property of the certificate and not of the value. A certificate that carries an arc more than its instance proves the planarity of a larger graph, and the planarity of the instance follows because a subgraph of a planar graph is planar; the check on the printed graph confirms that conclusion directly.

\paragraph{Suprema are not attained.} The values $\tfrac98$ and $(299-41\sqrt{41})/32$ are suprema of families and no member attains them. The members checked approach them from below: $\tfrac{4497}{4000}$ at $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ for the first, and $4558971/4000000$ at $t=\tfrac3{20}$, $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$, below the second by $\ngaplo$. A universal constant must be at least each supremum. The value $\tfrac98$ is not a ceiling for single instances: the four-interval members from $n=68$ on exceed it, the member at $n=68$ with $\tfrac{3060103}{2720000}$ by a hair, and the first certified point $\tfrac{8867}{7800}$ by more. The supremum $\tfrac98$ concerns the symmetric three-terminal template only \cite{Nik1}.

\paragraph{Where the instruments could reach.} The separation program and the frontier value are instruments for instances. The open problems of the series, such as whether the factor-two merger constant of \cite{Nik3} is finite, are statements about all instances, and the instruments give a test for a candidate instance without a handle on them. Cherniavskii and coauthors \cite{CKMS26} prove a cost-preserving rounding with an additive $O(\rho_{\max}\sqrt D)$ on hop-bounded instances; the instances of this paper have longest paths of at most a few arcs, and the comparison of the two constants is left to an evaluation that this paper does not make.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The record instances were read from a transcription of the printed sources that is common to the extraction and to this verification, so a transcription error shared by both would not be detected as a disagreement. The guards are that every value equals the printed claim to the last digit, that a change of one unit in the last printed digit of one demand is detected, and that the seven-vertex instance, decided separately,
gives $\tfrac{16}{15}$ through the same code. A shared misreading of the definition of the violation or of cost-goodness would affect the verification and the sources alike; the definitions follow \cite{TVZ24} and \cite{STVZ25}, and the normalisation of the constant of \cite{Nik1,Nik2,Nik3} is that of $\alpha^\star$ [V].
The restriction to simple paths is argued in Section~\ref{sec:instances}, not machine-checked.

\paragraph{External validity.} Every sweep is finite. The spine family was tested on a coarse box, the symmetric family on a grid with steps of $\tfrac1{16}$ in the fractions, the odd-cycle family on \nsixtotal\ members in three stages, and the two-terminal case on \ntwoterm\ instances of one shape. A member outside a box could behave differently, and no result is claimed outside the sampled space. The theorems of the sources are used as stated and were not re-proved. The refined 17-terminal instance is not printed in any of the three preprints; it was transcribed from the repository certificate that carries De la Fuente's refinement.

\paragraph{Construct validity and unconfirmed statements.} The cost convention of the figure of the ternary core in \cite{Nik3} is not printed in its caption; the convention of a unit cost $1/d_i$ on the early exit, used throughout the series, reproduces the printed value $(3-\eta)^2/8$, but the convention itself is [U] for that figure. The planarity of the instance of Figure~2 of \cite{BLRT26} was not checked [U]. A preprint on the discovery of the counterexample listed on SSRN under the identifier 7423078 could not be read (the host returned an access error), so it is not used.
The proof of the Dinitz--Garg--Goemans theorem is used as a statement and was not read in its original; the equivalence of \cite{MSS07} between the cost form and the convex-combination form is used only in its easy direction. The note of \cite{Muf26} is not peer reviewed. The realisation conditions of the construction of \cite{Afr26} are not imposed on the odd-cycle members, and the dates printed on its first version and in its identifier do not agree [U].

\paragraph{Scope of the computation.} The cutting-plane evaluator finished on instances of $\nkroutings$ routings in under nine minutes on one process; its behaviour on larger instances is not measured. The grid drawing of the planar record depends on the version of the graph library (\nnetworkx), and the drawing is re-checked for crossings at every build. Run times are those of one machine; the processor model was not recorded [U].

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The seven-vertex instance announced in 2026 is a counterexample to Goemans' unsplittable-flow cost conjecture, and it forces exactly $\tfrac{16}{15}\dmax$: the conjecture fails by one unit at $\dmax=15$. The separation program decides, for any instance, whether some nonnegative cost vector makes it a counterexample; on this instance its optimum is $\tfrac27$ and is attained by the published prices. No instance with one terminal is a counterexample, and a counterexample with two terminals would contain an arc of the cheapest paths whose flow is below the smaller demand. The frontier value, evaluated by an exact simplex and by a cutting-plane evaluator, shows that the published prices are optimal on all eleven record instances of Nikolenko and De la Fuente, whose values are reproduced to the last digit, and that two of them, the 17-terminal instances, are non-planar while the others are planar, each by a certificate that a separate checker accepts.
The symmetric three-terminal family of Muffatti forces $\tfrac98-\tfrac34\varepsilon$ at every $\varepsilon$ tested, and Afrasyab's odd-cycle family on $C_5$ never exceeds $1$ on \nsixtotal\ members.

Four questions remain open. Whether a counterexample with two terminals exists. Whether the factor-two merger constant of Open Problem~11.1 of \cite{Nik3} is finite, which would give a universal cost-preserving additive constant; only $K\ge2.5652$ is known. Where the planar constant lies in the interval from $1.1735$ to $2$. And whether the supremum of the odd-cycle family on $C_5$ is $1$. A new lower bound needs a new instance or a new family, since re-pricing the known instances gains nothing.
