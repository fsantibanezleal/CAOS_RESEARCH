\begin{abstract}
Alp\"oge's explicit Keller map on $\C^3$, with a three-point fibre, refutes the Jacobian conjecture in every dimension $N\ge3$. Seven neighbouring conjectures are tied to it by published implications whose dimension indices decide what the map refutes. We locate each implication in a primary text, record its theorem and index, and close the implications under composition into a status table with an evidence mark per entry. Exact computations over $\Q$ and $\Q(i)$ check the map and its fibre, a homogeneous-type test, a dimension-15 homogeneous witness, a dimension-48 gradient witness whose Hessian-nilpotent quartic has \npotentialmon\ monomials, and Long's rank-two Poisson map. The Dixmier and Poisson conjectures fail at every index $n\ge3$, and the Poisson conjecture also at $n=2$. The Mathieu conjecture fails for $SU(N)$ with $N\ge15$ and, by Long, for $SU(2)$. The Gaussian moments conjecture fails for $n\ge3$, and the vanishing, Hessian and symmetric forms fail with explicit witnesses. Every check is exact, so no interval applies, and each negative control fires. Mathieu's original paper was not located, so that row rests on an expository rendering. Dixmier at ranks 1 and 2, Poisson at index 1, Mathieu for $3\le N\le14$ and the two-variable Jacobian conjecture stay open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

On 2026-07-20, Levent Alp\"oge announced an explicit counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture in dimension 3, found working with the language model Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic). % voice-ok: external attribution, as the announcement states it
The map is a Keller map $F:\C^3\to\C^3$ with $\det JF=\ndetF$ that sends three distinct points to one value. It refutes the Jacobian conjecture $JC(N)$ for every $N\ge3$ and leaves $JC(2)$ open \cite{Alpoge2026}. The credit for every consequence documented here belongs to that single map.

What the map decides about the neighbouring conjectures is not immediate, because the published links between the Jacobian conjecture and its neighbours carry dimension indices. The Dixmier conjecture of index $n$ implies $JC(n)$, while $JC(2n)$ implies the Dixmier conjecture of index $n$ \cite{BKK2007}. A counterexample in dimension 3 therefore falsifies $DC(n)$ for every $n\ge3$ through the first implication, and decides nothing about $DC(2)$ through the second, whose premise $JC(4)$ is false and so silent. The Mathieu conjecture raises a second difficulty. The located proof that $MC(SU(N))$ implies a statement about $\C^N$ treats Keller maps of the form $x-h$ with $h$ homogeneous, and the counterexample, of total degree 7, is not of that form. A first list of consequences \cite{Zhang2026} states the fixed-dimensional form of the Mathieu implication, derives the failure of the Mathieu conjecture for $SU(3)$, and obtains the other consequences only in some unspecified dimension.

What is missing is one table that gives, for each neighbouring conjecture, the implication with its index as the source states it, the theorem in which it was located, the dimensions that the implication decides, an explicit witness where one exists, and an evidence mark that separates what a machine checked from what was read, derived or only claimed. This paper supplies that table and the computations that stand behind it.

The method has two parts. First, the published implications are transcribed with their indices from the primary texts and closed under composition by a finite propagation of truth values (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). Second, five exact procedures check the objects on which the table rests: the fibre of a polynomial map, a test for affine homogeneous type, the transport of a collision through the de Bondt--van den Essen symmetrization, a re-implementation of de Bondt's homogenization, and the Poisson-endomorphism check of Long's map (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). Each reduction that the paper composes on top of a located statement is proved in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

The contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A status table of seven conjectures (Table~\ref{tab:status}), each row with its implication and index, the source theorem located, an evidence mark, the explicit witness with its dimension, and the open indices.
\item An implication graph (Figure~\ref{fig:graph}) and an index map (Figure~\ref{fig:index}) built from the same data by a script of the paper's folder.
\item Five procedures stated in pseudocode with their inputs, arithmetic domain, software and measured cost (Table~\ref{tab:cost}), and a proof of soundness for every reduction used.
\item Exact checks of every explicit witness: the map and its fibre, the dimension-15 homogeneous map, the dimension-48 gradient map with its collision and potential, and Long's Poisson map, together with the validation routes and negative controls of Sections~\ref{sec:validation} and~\ref{sec:ablation}.
\item A scope statement for the Mathieu row: the located proofs do not reach $SU(N)$ for $3\le N\le14$ through the counterexample, and the failure for $N\ge15$ rests on an explicit homogeneous map.
\end{enumerate}

The main result is the status table. The Dixmier and Poisson conjectures are false at every index $n\ge3$, the Poisson conjecture is false at $n=2$ as well, and only index 1 remains open there. The Mathieu conjecture is false for $SU(N)$ with $N\ge\nhomdim$ and for $SU(2)$. The Gaussian moments conjecture is false for $n\ge3$. The vanishing, Image, Hessian and symmetric forms are false, with explicit witnesses in dimension \nwitnessdim\ for the vanishing and Hessian forms. All exact checks pass, and every negative control fails as it must.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

The related work falls into six groups: the conjecture and its classical reductions, the symmetric and Hessian formulations, the Weyl-algebra and Poisson formulations, the Mathieu conjecture, the Gaussian moments and Image conjectures, and the literature that followed the counterexample. For each work we state what it assumes, what it gives and, where the records allow, where it stops. Marks are defined in Section~\ref{sec:marks}.

\paragraph{The conjecture and its classical reductions.} Keller's 1939 paper on integral Cremona transformations is the classical origin of the question and proves the birational case \cite{Keller1939} [U]. The two-variable statement appears already in an 1884 paper of Kraus, whose argument fails at the control of ramification at infinity, still the main obstacle to algebro-geometric approaches \cite{RodriguezDiaz2026} [U]. Wang proved the case of maps of degree two in every dimension \cite{Wang1980} [U]. Moh verified the two-variable case up to degree 100 \cite{Moh1983} [U]; the bound is specific to $N=2$ and says nothing about the counterexample, which has degree 7 in dimension 3. Bass, Connell and Wright reduce the conjecture to cubic homogeneous maps $x+N$ with nilpotent Jacobian; each monomial-splitting step adds two variables, the unipotent step doubles the dimension and the homogenization adds one \cite{BCW1982} [V]. Druzkowski reduces further to the cubic-linear form $F_i=x_i+(\sum_ja_{ij}x_j)^3$ \cite{Druzkowski1983} [U], and Gorni and Zampieri pair a cubic homogeneous map in dimension $n$ with a cubic-linear map in dimension $n+q$, each invertible exactly when the other is \cite{GorniZampieri1997} [U]. Pinchuk gave a map with nowhere-vanishing but non-constant Jacobian determinant that is not injective on $\mathbb R^2$, which refutes the strong real conjecture and leaves the Jacobian conjecture itself untouched \cite{Pinchuk1994} [U]. The standard monograph is \cite{vdE2000} [U]. None of these results rules out the counterexample: the reductions convert a counterexample to a normal form in higher dimension and never bounded the search in dimension 3.

\paragraph{Symmetric and Hessian formulations.} De Bondt and van den Essen reduce the conjecture to maps $x+H$ with symmetric nilpotent $JH$, equivalently to gradients of Hessian-nilpotent quartics, through a construction that doubles the dimension and preserves non-invertibility in both directions \cite{dBvdE2005} [V]. Their companion papers formulate the symmetric dependence problems and prove the symmetric case for $n\le4$ without homogeneity and for $n=5$ with $H$ homogeneous \cite{dBvdE2004, dBvdE2005b} [U]; van den Essen and Washburn treat $n\le4$ with $H$ homogeneous \cite{vdEW2004} [U]. Meng obtained a near-simultaneous reduction to gradient maps $\nabla f$ with $\det h(f)\in\C^\times$; the version of de Bondt and van den Essen is sharper because it preserves nilpotency, and neither lowers the dimension \cite{Meng2006} [U]. De Bondt later strengthened the structural target to maps whose Jacobian has all the dihedral symmetries of the square \cite{dBSym2014} [U]. Zhao proved that the vanishing conjecture for homogeneous Hessian-nilpotent quartics is equivalent to the Jacobian conjecture in all dimensions (Theorem~7.2 of \cite{Zhao2004} [V]) and extended the program to generalized vanishing statements \cite{Zhao2007} [U]. The symmetric positive results fix the smallest dimension in which a witness can live; they do not construct one.

\paragraph{Weyl-algebra and Poisson formulations.} Tsuchimoto and, independently with a different proof, Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich proved that $JC(2n)$ implies the Dixmier conjecture of index $n$ \cite{Tsuchimoto2005, BKK2007} [U] [V]. Adjamagbo and van den Essen placed the Poisson conjecture between them, $JC(2n)\Rightarrow PC(n)\Rightarrow DC(n)\Rightarrow JC(n)$, in every characteristic \cite{AvdE2007} [V]. Zheglov claims a proof of the Dixmier conjecture for the first Weyl algebra \cite{Zheglov2024} [U]. Kanel-Belov and Fedorov prove that $JC(2)$ implies the generalized Dixmier statement for the first Weyl algebra, and that the converse holds under an additional hypothesis on holonomic lifts; they announce no counterexample to $DC(2)$ or $JC(2)$ \cite{KBF2026} [V]. Long's rank-two Poisson map is the first explicit failure of the Poisson conjecture below the chain's reach \cite{LongPoisson2026} [V].

\paragraph{The Mathieu conjecture.} Mathieu proved that his conjecture, taken for all compact connected Lie groups, implies the Jacobian conjecture \cite{Mathieu1997} [U]; the paper was not located. Duistermaat and van der Kallen proved the abelian case \cite{DvdK1998} [U]. Five later texts state the implication without a fixed dimension \cite{vdEMathieu, vdEWZ, DingsKoelink, Zhao2009, MuegerTuset} [V]. Zwart's review is the only located proof of a fixed-dimensional form, and only for maps $x-h$ with $h$ homogeneous \cite{Zwart2025} [V]. Long gave explicit functions on $SU(2)$ that violate the conjecture \cite{LongSU2} [V]; Dings and Koelink and M\"uger and Tuset had studied the $SU(2)$ case before \cite{DingsKoelink, MuegerTuset} [V].

\paragraph{Gaussian moments and Image conjectures.} Derksen, van den Essen and Zhao proved that the Gaussian moments conjecture for all $n$ implies the Jacobian conjecture and that it holds for $n=1$ \cite{DEZ2017} [V]. Long gave explicit polynomials in three variables that violate it, so it fails for every $n\ge3$ \cite{Long2026} [V], and Wilson claims a proof for $n=2$ \cite{Wilson2026} [U]. Van den Essen introduced the Image conjecture, which implies the vanishing conjecture \cite{vdE2010} [U].

\paragraph{After the counterexample.} The announcement \cite{Alpoge2026} [U] is not a paper; the map in it is checked here [MV]. Thompson published an explicit cubic homogeneous form in dimension 24, with a collision pair, which supplies the input of Section~\ref{sec:fortyeight} \cite{Thompson2026} [MV]. De Bondt gave homogeneous and vanishing-conjecture counterexamples in smaller dimensions, among them a homogeneous map in dimension 15 and vanishing-conjecture failures in dimensions 28 and 30 over the Gaussian integers \cite{deBondt2026} [V]. Van den Essen recovers the map, up to linear changes, by an elementary search guided by a reducible third component, and recalls Magnus's criterion that a planar Keller map with a component of prime degree is invertible \cite{vdE2026} [V]. The first list of consequences is a web note \cite{Zhang2026} [U]. The companion papers treat the three-dimensional structure and the planar case \cite{FoundationalPaper, PlanarPaper}.

\paragraph{Where this paper stands.} No work above tabulates, conjecture by conjecture, the indices that the counterexample decides. This paper adds the table, the proofs that its reductions are sound, and exact checks of the objects on which it rests; it proposes no new implication. The explicit witness in dimension 48 is not the smallest known; its use is that every step is checked exactly here.

\section{Problem formulation and conventions}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Maps, conjectures and indices}\label{sec:defs}

A Keller map is a polynomial map $F:\C^N\to\C^N$ whose Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant. The Jacobian conjecture $JC(N)$ asserts that every Keller map of $\C^N$ has a polynomial inverse:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jc}
\det JF\in\C^\times\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists\,G\ \text{polynomial with}\ G\circ F=\mathrm{id}.
\end{equation}
A Keller map with two distinct points of equal image has no inverse of any kind, so refuting $JC(N)$ needs one such pair. Let $A_n$ be the $n$-th Weyl algebra. The Dixmier conjecture $DC(n)$ asserts that every algebra endomorphism of $A_n$ is an automorphism \cite{BKK2007}. The canonical Poisson algebra $P_n$ is the polynomial algebra in $2n$ variables with a classical bracket; $PC(n)$ asserts that every Poisson endomorphism of $P_n$ is an automorphism \cite{AvdE2007}. For the chart of Long's example the bracket is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bracket}
\{f,g\}=f_pg_x-f_xg_p+f_zg_q-f_qg_z\qquad\text{in the order }(x,q,p,z),
\end{equation}
so that $\{p,x\}=\{z,q\}=1$ \cite{LongPoisson2026}. The Mathieu conjecture $MC(K)$ concerns regular functions $F,G$ on a compact connected Lie group $K$ with Haar measure $dg$: in Long's counterexample on $SU(2)$ the functions satisfy $\int F^n\,dg=0$ and $\int F^nG\,dg\ne0$ for every $n\ge1$, which contradicts the conjecture \cite{LongSU2}. The full statement of the conjecture is not transcribed in the computations of this paper and is not used beyond the implications below. The Gaussian moments conjecture $GMC(n)$ concerns polynomials $P,Q$ in $n$ independent standard real Gaussian variables: the conjecture is that $E(P^m)=0$ for all $m\ge1$ forces $E(QP^m)=0$ for large $m$ \cite{DEZ2017}, and Long's polynomials have $E(P^m)=0$ and $E(QP^m)=m!\ne0$ for every $m\ge1$, which contradicts it for $n=3$ \cite{Long2026}.

For the vanishing conjecture let $\Delta=\sum_i\partial_i^2$. A polynomial $P$ is Hessian nilpotent (HN) when its Hessian matrix is nilpotent. Zhao's Conjecture~7.1 states that for every HN polynomial $P$ the deformed inversion pair $Q_t$ is a polynomial in $t$ and $z$, more precisely that $\Delta^kP^{k+1}=0$ for $k\gg0$ \cite{Zhao2004}. The Hessian conjecture $HC(n)$ of de Bondt and van den Essen states that for $f\in\C[x]$ with nilpotent Hessian $h(f)$ the map $(x_1+f_{x_1},\dots,x_n+f_{x_n})$ is invertible \cite{dBvdE2005}. The Image conjecture is used only through its implication to the vanishing conjecture \cite{vdE2010}. The dimension index of each statement is its rank $n$ for $DC$, its number $n$ of canonical pairs (so $2n$ variables) for $PC$, the group $SU(N)$ for $MC$, and the number of Gaussian variables for $GMC$.

\subsection{Evidence marks}\label{sec:marks}

Every result and every literature statement carries one of five marks. A statement is [MV] (machine-verified) when an exact computation of this paper's archived record checked it, [D] (derived) when it follows by the reasoning of Section~\ref{sec:theory} from statements that carry other marks, and [C] (conjectural) when it is open. A literature statement is [V] when its text was located and the statement was read in the primary source, and [U] when the source was not located or the statement was not verified here. A claimed proof that was not checked, such as Zheglov's and Wilson's, is [U]. A row of the table can combine marks, one per ingredient. The mark never states more than its weakest ingredient allows: a derived consequence of a [U] implication is not promoted. In Table~\ref{tab:status} the marks of the implication, of the witness and of the closure are given separately in one cell, so that a reader can see which ingredient of a row is checked here and which is only read.

\subsection{The closure of the located implications}\label{sec:closure}

The implications of Table~\ref{tab:status} that involve only $JC$, $DC$ and $PC$ are finite in number once the indices are bounded. Let $V$ assign to each atom $JC(n)$, $DC(n)$ and $PC(n)$ a value in $\{\text{true},\text{false},\text{undecided}\}$. The located rules are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rules}
\begin{gathered}
DC(n)\Rightarrow JC(n),\qquad JC(2n)\Rightarrow DC(n),\qquad JC(2n)\Rightarrow PC(n)\Rightarrow DC(n),\\
X(n)\Rightarrow X(m)\quad(n>m,\ X\in\{JC,DC\}).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The first rule is the well-known direction and the monotonicity is stated in the introduction of \cite{BKK2007}; the second is their Theorem~1; the third and fourth are Theorem~7 of \cite{AvdE2007}. The ground facts are $JC(1)$ true, $JC(n)$ false for $n\ge3$ and $JC(2)$ open. A true premise makes its conclusion true, and a false conclusion makes its premise false; the closure is the fixed point of both propagations. The closure is tabulated for $n\le\nclosuremax$, with $JC$ carried to $2\nclosuremax$ so that $JC(2n)$ exists for every tabulated $n$. A statement left undecided is undecided by these implications and not by the literature at large. The closure is also run with $JC(2)$ set to true and to false, which shows what each answer to the planar question would decide. Closure is a lower bound on what is decided: Section~\ref{sec:poissonresult} shows a value, $PC(2)$, that the rules leave undecided and an explicit construction decides.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

Five procedures carry every computation of this paper. All of them run in exact arithmetic over $\Q$ or $\Q(i)$ with no floating-point step, in the algebra system sympy and the Python standard library; the status table, the figures and every number of the text are generated from the archived artifacts by the script \texttt{make\_status.py} of the paper's folder. Table~\ref{tab:cost} states, for each procedure, the computation that ran it, its input, the arithmetic domain, the software and the cost measured in that run. Costs are reported as measured sizes and times, not as asymptotic bounds, because every Gr\"obner computation here has a few polynomials in three or four variables. Each procedure returns an object to which the paper attaches a mark, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} proves that each reduction applied to the object is sound.

\subsection{A1: the fibre of a polynomial map by a lexicographic Gr\"obner basis}\label{sec:a1}

Given a polynomial map $F=(F_1,\dots,F_m)$ over $\Q$ and a target $t\in\Q^m$, the fibre $F^{-1}(t)$ is the zero set of the ideal $I_t=\langle F_1-t_1,\dots,F_m-t_m\rangle$. A reduced lexicographic basis of $I_t$ eliminates the variables one at a time, so the fibre is read off a univariate eliminant followed by back-substitution. For the counterexample $F$ of Section~\ref{sec:witnessmap} and the target $(-\tfrac14,0,0)$, computation \EXP{001} obtains the basis
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basis}
\tfrac13(3x+2y),\qquad \tfrac1{12}(12y^2-4z-1),\qquad \tfrac12\,y\,(2z-13),\qquad \tfrac18(2z-13)(4z+1),
\end{equation}
whose last element is the eliminant in $z$ with roots $\tfrac{13}2$ and $-\tfrac14$. At $z=\tfrac{13}2$ the second element gives $y=\pm\tfrac32$ and the first $x=-\tfrac23y$; at $z=-\tfrac14$ it gives $y=0$ and $x=0$. The fibre consists of the three points $(0,0,-\tfrac14)$, $(1,-\tfrac32,\tfrac{13}2)$ and $(-1,\tfrac32,\tfrac{13}2)$.

The basis fixes the fibre set but not its multiplicities. Computation \EXP{144} adds a radical certificate: when $I_t$ is zero-dimensional, $\dim_\Q\Q[x]/I_t$ equals the number of standard monomials, those not divisible by any leading monomial of the basis, and if that number equals the number of distinct complex solutions then $I_t$ is radical, so every point has multiplicity one. Algorithm~\ref{alg:a1} lists the steps; steps 5 to 7 were run in \EXP{144} and not in \EXP{001}. The arithmetic domain is $\Q$, so a point of the fibre is exact. The cost of the basis for Long's map, four variables and five basis elements, was \npoissongb\ s.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{A1, the fibre of a polynomial map by a lexicographic basis.}\label{alg:a1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require polynomials $F_1,\dots,F_m\in\Q[x_1,\dots,x_m]$ and a target $t\in\Q^m$
\State $I\gets\langle F_1-t_1,\dots,F_m-t_m\rangle$; $\;G\gets$ reduced lex Gr\"obner basis of $I$ over $\Q$
\If{$G=\{1\}$} \Return the empty fibre \EndIf
\If{some variable has no pure power among the leading monomials of $G$} \Return ``positive dimension'' \EndIf
\State $S\gets$ complex solutions of the triangular system $G=0$ by back-substitution from the eliminant
\State $d\gets$ number of standard monomials of $G$, that is $\dim_\Q\Q[x]/I$
\If{$d=|S|$} \State record ``$I$ is radical'' \EndIf
\For{$s\in S$} \State verify $F(s)=t$ exactly by substitution \EndFor
\State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A2: the test for affine homogeneous type}\label{sec:a2}

A Keller map is of affine homogeneous type when $F=A\circ(x-h)\circ B$ with $A,B$ affine bijections and every component of $h$ homogeneous of one degree $d$. The Mathieu implication of Zwart treats exactly maps $x-h$, so the question is whether the counterexample can be brought to that form without adding variables. Let $J_2(F)$ be the ideal generated by all second partial derivatives of all components of $F$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:j2}
J_2(F)=\big\langle\,\partial_a\partial_bF_i\ :\ 1\le a\le b\le m,\ 1\le i\le m\,\big\rangle .
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:homtype} below shows that a map of affine homogeneous type of degree $d\ge3$ has a point where all second partials vanish, that is $V(J_2(F))\ne\emptyset$. The test therefore computes a Gr\"obner basis of $J_2(F)$ and answers ``not of affine homogeneous type'' when the basis is $\{1\}$, and ``inconclusive'' otherwise. The test is one-sided: a nonempty zero set does not prove homogeneous type.

For the counterexample the components have total degrees $7$, $6$ and $4$, so any affine homogeneous form would have $d=7$. Of the 18 possible second partials, \nsecondgens\ are nonzero, and their ideal has the basis $\{1\}$. An independent certificate that does not use a Gr\"obner routine is checked by substitution (algorithm~\ref{alg:a2}, step 5): from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b3}
\partial_x\partial_yF_3=-6x,\qquad \partial_x^2F_3\big|_{x=0}=-6y,\qquad \partial_y^2F_1\big|_{x=y=0}=8,
\end{equation}
a common zero would need $x=0$, then $y=0$, and then $8=0$. The same test, applied to the 24-variable cubic form, takes the opposite course: each component of $H$ is homogeneous cubic, so $x+H=x-h$ with $h=-H$ homogeneous, and no search is needed.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{A2, the test for affine homogeneous type.}\label{alg:a2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require components $F_1,\dots,F_m\in\Q[x_1,\dots,x_m]$
\State $D\gets\max_i\deg F_i$; \textbf{if} $D\le2$ \textbf{then} stop (the argument needs $D\ge3$)
\State $L\gets$ list of the nonzero polynomials $\partial_a\partial_bF_i$ for all $i$ and $a\le b$
\State $G\gets$ Gr\"obner basis of $\langle L\rangle$ over $\Q$ (graded reverse lexicographic order)
\If{$G=\{1\}$} \State answer ``not of affine homogeneous type''
\Else \State answer ``inconclusive'' \EndIf
\State check a hand certificate by substitution when one is available (Equation~\eqref{eq:b3})
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The arithmetic domain is $\Q$ and the software is sympy. The time of this run was not recorded in the artifact; the planned budget for the basis was 10 s, gated at 120 s, with the substitution certificate as the fallback.

\subsection{A3: symmetrization and the transport of a collision}\label{sec:a3}

Let $H:\Q^n\to\Q^n$ be a polynomial map with $JH$ nilpotent and let $u\ne v$ satisfy $u+H(u)=v+H(v)$. The de Bondt--van den Essen construction \cite{dBvdE2005} adds $n$ variables $y$ and sets
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potential}
f_H(x,y)=-i\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_j(x+iy)\,y_j,\qquad S(x,y)=(x-iy,\,y),
\end{equation}
with the identity, verified symbolically in all $2n$ components,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conj}
S^{-1}\circ(\mathrm{id}+\nabla f_H)\circ S\;=\;\big(x+H(x),\;y+JH(x)^{t}y-iH(x)\big).
\end{equation}
The map $\widetilde F=\mathrm{id}+\nabla f_H$ is then a symmetric Keller map. A collision of $x+H$ transports to a collision of $\widetilde F$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:collision}
w=i\,\big(I+JH(v)^{t}\big)^{-1}(u-v),\qquad p_1=(u,0),\qquad p_2=(v-iw,\,w),
\end{equation}
where the inverse is the finite Neumann sum $\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-JH(v)^t)^k$ and $\nu$ is the nilpotency index of $JH$. Nilpotency of $JH$ is established by exact powers: the powers $(JH)^k$ are computed as sparse matrices of polynomials stored as dictionaries of \texttt{Fraction} coefficients, and the traces $\mathrm{Tr}(JH^k)$ are checked as an independent consistency test through the Newton identities. Algorithm~\ref{alg:a3} lists the steps.

The input is Thompson's cubic homogeneous form on $\Q^{24}$ with 54 monomials and a rational collision pair, matched to its SHA-256 digest before use (Table~\ref{tab:hashes}). The arithmetic domain is $\Q(i)$ for the potential and the collision and $\Q$ for the powers of $JH$. The chain of products had term counts \njhone, \njhtwo, \njhfour, \njheight, \njhsixteen\ and \njhseventeen\ for the powers $k=1,2,4,8,16,17$; the power $(JH)^{\nnilindex}$ vanishes (Figure~\ref{fig:chain}). The potential $f_H$ has \npotentialmon\ monomials over $\Q(i)$, and $\Delta P_\star^2$ for $P_\star=-f_H$ has \ndeltaterms\ terms.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{A3, symmetrization of $x+H$ and transport of a collision.}\label{alg:a3}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $H$ on $\Q^n$ with every component homogeneous, and $u\ne v$ with $u+H(u)=v+H(v)$
\State check the SHA-256 digests of the input files; check homogeneity and the collision exactly
\State $P\gets I$; \textbf{for} $k=1,2,\dots$ \textbf{do} $P\gets P\cdot JH$ (sparse, exact), recording the number of terms and, at selected $k$, $\mathrm{Tr}\,P$; \textbf{until} $P=0$; let $\nu\gets k$
\State $f\gets-i\sum_jH_j(x+iy)y_j$ expanded over $\Q(i)$; check that $f$ is a homogeneous quartic when $H$ is cubic
\State check the identity~\eqref{eq:conj} in all $2n$ components
\State $B\gets\sum_{k=0}^{\nu-1}(-JH(v)^t)^k$; $\;w\gets i\,B\,(u-v)$; $\;p_1\gets(u,0)$; $\;p_2\gets(v-iw,w)$
\State check $p_1\ne p_2$ and $\widetilde F(p_1)=\widetilde F(p_2)$ in all $2n$ components, exactly over $\Q(i)$
\State $P_\star\gets-f$; count its monomials and the terms of $\Delta P_\star^2$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A4: the homogenization of de Bondt}\label{sec:a4}

De Bondt starts from a volume-normalized form of the counterexample, $\det JF=1$, with identity linear part, and reduces the degrees of its components with new variables until every component has the form $x_i+H_i$ with $H_i$ homogeneous of one degree. Computation \EXP{138} re-implements this construction in sympy from the description of the paper and the ancillary file read as text only, and verifies the output exactly. The input is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalised}
F=\big(x_1-3x_1^2x_2-x_1^3x_3,\ \ 3v^2x_1x_3+(9v^2-6v-2)x_2,\ \ v^3x_3+(6v^2+2v)x_2^2\big),\qquad v=1+2x_1x_2,
\end{equation}
with $\det JF=1$ and $F(0,1,-8)=F(-1,1,4)=(0,1,0)$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:a4} lists the steps. The collision is carried along the construction: each new coordinate takes the value that makes its component vanish at both points, so equal images persist, and the last variable replaces the scaling parameter $t$ at the value 1.

The output is $G=x+H$ on $\C^{\nhomdim}$ with every $H_i$ homogeneous of degree \nhomdeg. The check does not depend on the correctness of the algorithm, because the output is verified directly: $(JH)^{\nhomnil}=0$ in exact arithmetic, so $\det JG=\det(I+JH)=1$; the two integer points $(0,1,-8,0,\dots,0,1)$ and $(-1,1,4,-4,-1,48,12,-36,-12,32,8,-40,-12,12,1)$ are distinct and have equal images; and every $H_i$ is homogeneous of degree \nhomdeg\ or zero. The whole computation took \nhomseconds\ s. The negative control adds $x_2^2$ to the second component of $F$: the determinant becomes the non-constant polynomial $\nhomctrl$, so the input check of step 1 rejects the perturbed map.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{A4, homogenization of a Keller map with a collision.}\label{alg:a4}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $F$ on $\Q^n$ with identity linear part and $\det JF$ constant; points $P_1\ne P_2$ with $F(P_1)=F(P_2)$
\State check $\det JF$ is a nonzero constant and $F(P_1)=F(P_2)$ exactly; $d\gets\max_i\deg F_i$
\State $G_i\gets t^{-(d-2)}F_i(t^{d-2}x)$ for all $i$ (expanded, a polynomial in $x$ and $t$)
\For{each component $G_i$} \While{$\deg G_i>d$ in $(x,t)$}
\State split off the top-degree part: $G_i\gets g$ and append a new coordinate $x_{m+1}$ with component $h=(\text{top part})/t^{\deg-d}+x_{m+1}$
\EndWhile \EndFor
\State lift each $P_j$: scale by $t^{-(d-2)}$ and give every new coordinate the value that makes its component vanish at $P_j$
\State append the variable $s$ with component $s$, replace $t$ by $s$, and set the last coordinate of both points to $1$
\State check: every $H_i=G_i-x_i$ is homogeneous of degree $d$ or zero; powers $(JH)^k$ until zero; equal images at the two points
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A5: the Poisson-endomorphism check of Long's map}\label{sec:a5}

Long's map $(x,q,p,z)\mapsto(R,T,D,S)$ is given by equations (12) to (19) of arXiv:2608.23777v1 as seven auxiliary polynomials $\beta$, $y$, $R$, $S$, $T$, $H$ and $D$ over $\Q$. Computation \EXP{144} transcribed them from the page images of the PDF, whose SHA-256 digest is pinned in Table~\ref{tab:hashes}; the run stops with a distinct exit code when the digest differs. The transcription is tested by the sizes that the paper prints under equation (19): the seven expressions have \nsizebetaterms, \nsizeyterms, \nsizeRterms, \nsizeSterms, \nsizeTterms, \nsizeHterms\ and \nsizeDterms\ nonzero terms and total degrees \nsizebetadeg, \nsizeydeg, \nsizeRdeg, \nsizeSdeg, \nsizeTdeg, \nsizeHdeg\ and \nsizeDdeg, which the transcription reproduces term for term (Figure~\ref{fig:poisson}), and $R$ expands to $x(2-3xq)$.

The Poisson property is checked by two routes. The first computes symbolically the bracket matrix of $(R,T,D,S)$ and compares it with that of $(x,q,p,z)$. The second evaluates $JF\,\Pi\,JF^t-\Pi$, with $\Pi$ the matrix of the brackets of the coordinates, at \npoissonpts\ random rational points with exact fractions (seed \npoissonseed). The determinant is computed by the Berkowitz method in sympy and by fraction-based elimination at the same points. The fibre over $(R,T,D,S)=(0,\tfrac18,0,0)$ is obtained with algorithm~\ref{alg:a1}, and the reducibility of $R$ is tested by factorization over $\Q$. Two perturbation controls rerun the bracket check on a modified input. The whole run took \npoissonseconds\ s, of which the symbolic brackets took \npoissonbrk\ s and the determinant \npoissondet\ s.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{A5, exact check that a map is a non-automorphic Poisson endomorphism.}\label{alg:a5}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the PDF with its pinned SHA-256; the bracket convention~\eqref{eq:bracket}
\State \textbf{abort} if the digest differs; transcribe the polynomials; compare term counts and degrees with the printed table
\State compute the symbolic bracket matrix of $(R,T,D,S)$; require it to equal that of $(x,q,p,z)$
\State at 50 random rational points (fixed seed) require $JF\,\Pi\,JF^t-\Pi=0$ in \texttt{Fraction} arithmetic
\State compute $\det JF$ symbolically (Berkowitz) and at the same points; require the value $1$
\State apply A1 to the target $(0,\tfrac18,0,0)$; require three solutions and quotient dimension $3$
\State factor $R$ over $\Q$; require two non-constant factors
\State repeat steps 2 and 3 on a modified $D$ and on a modified coefficient of $H$; require both to fail
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Theory: soundness of the reductions}\label{sec:theory}

The propositions of this section justify each reduction that the paper composes on top of a located statement or an exact output. The proofs are short because every ingredient is either a located theorem or an exact check.

\begin{proposition}[Dummy variables]\label{prop:dummy}
If $F:\C^3\to\C^3$ is a Keller map with two distinct points of equal image, then for every $N\ge3$ the map $F\times\mathrm{id}_{N-3}$ is a Keller map of $\C^N$ with the same property. Hence $JC(N)$ is false for all $N\ge3$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Jacobian matrix is block diagonal with blocks $JF$ and the identity, so its determinant is $\det JF$. If $F(a)=F(b)$ with $a\ne b$, the points $(a,c)$ and $(b,c)$ for any $c$ have equal image. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Closure]\label{prop:closure}
Every value that the closure of Section~\ref{sec:closure} assigns is a consequence of the ground facts and the rules~\eqref{eq:rules} by modus ponens and modus tollens. In particular $DC(n)$ and $PC(n)$ are false for every $n\ge3$, and the closure leaves $DC(1)$, $DC(2)$ and $PC(1)$ undecided.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A value is assigned only by propagation along a rule, forwards from a true premise or backwards from a false conclusion, so each assignment is a chain of such steps. For $n\ge3$, $JC(n)$ is false, $DC(n)\Rightarrow JC(n)$ makes $DC(n)$ false, and $PC(n)\Rightarrow DC(n)$ makes $PC(n)$ false. A value for $DC(2)$ would need a true premise among the rules that conclude $DC(2)$, which are $JC(4)\Rightarrow DC(2)$, $PC(2)\Rightarrow DC(2)$ and $DC(n)\Rightarrow DC(2)$ for $n>2$, or a false conclusion among the rules that start from $DC(2)$, which are $DC(2)\Rightarrow JC(2)$ and $DC(2)\Rightarrow DC(1)$. The premises $JC(4)$ and $DC(n)$ with $n>2$ are false, $PC(2)$ is undecided or, with Long's map, false, and the conclusions $JC(2)$ and $DC(1)$ are undecided; so none can force a value. The same listing holds for $DC(1)$ and $PC(1)$, because no located rule leads from $PC(2)$ to $PC(1)$. The conditional closures of computation \EXP{136} confirm that both values of $JC(2)$ are consistent with the rules. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Homogeneous-type obstruction]\label{prop:homtype}
Let $F=A\circ(x-h)\circ B$ with $A,B$ affine bijections of $\C^N$ and every $h_i$ homogeneous of the same degree $d\ge3$, not all zero. Then $\max_i\deg F_i=d$, and all second partial derivatives of all $F_i$ vanish at the point $b=B^{-1}(0)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The map $y\mapsto y-h(y)$ has Taylor expansion at $0$ with terms of degree $1$ and $d$ only. Composition with the affine map $B$, centred at $b$, gives $B(x-b)-h(B(x-b))$, a polynomial in $x-b$ with terms of degree $1$ and $d$ only, and composition with the affine map $A$ takes linear combinations of components and adds a constant. So the Taylor expansion of $F$ at $b$ has no terms of degrees $2,\dots,d-1$, which includes degree $2$ because $d\ge3$. The maximal degree is $d$, because a linear combination of components cannot exceed the maximum and the inverse combination recovers it. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For the counterexample, $d=\max_i\deg F_i=7\ge3$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:homtype} applies, and the basis $\{1\}$ of $J_2(F)$ shows that no such $b$ exists.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Symmetrization]\label{prop:sym}
Let $H:\C^n\to\C^n$ be polynomial with $JH$ nilpotent. Then $\widetilde F=\mathrm{id}+\nabla f_H$ satisfies the conjugation identity~\eqref{eq:conj}, $\det J\widetilde F=1$, and $\widetilde F$ is injective if and only if $x+H$ is. If $u+H(u)=v+H(v)$ with $u\ne v$, then the points $p_1,p_2$ of~\eqref{eq:collision} are distinct and $\widetilde F(p_1)=\widetilde F(p_2)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity~\eqref{eq:conj} and the characteristic-polynomial identity $\det(zI-h(f_H))\big|_{S(x,y)}=\det(zI-JH)\det(zI-JH^{t})$ are proved in \cite{dBvdE2005} for every $H$ with nilpotent $JH$, and computation \EXP{041} checks the first identically in all 48 components for the $H$ of this paper. The Hessian of $f_H$ is then nilpotent, so $\det J\widetilde F=\det(I+h(f_H))=1$. In the conjugated form the first block is $x+H(x)$ and the second is affine in $y$ with matrix $I+JH(x)^t$, invertible for every $x$ because $JH(x)$ is nilpotent; a map of this triangular shape is injective exactly when its first block is. For the collision, the first block takes the value $u+H(u)=v+H(v)$ at $(u,0)$ and $(v,w)$. The second block takes $-iH(u)$ at $(u,0)$ and $w+JH(v)^tw-iH(v)$ at $(v,w)$, which are equal because $(I+JH(v)^t)w=i(u-v)=i\,(H(v)-H(u))$ by the definition of $w$ and the equality of the images. Since $S$ is a bijection, $p_1=S(u,0)$ and $p_2=S(v,w)$ are distinct and collide. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Mathieu transfer and padding]\label{prop:mathieu}
Suppose Theorems~4.23 and~4.16 of \cite{Zwart2025} hold as stated: $MC(SU(N))$ implies that every Keller map $x-h$ of $\C^N$ with $h$ homogeneous is invertible. If $G=x-h$ is a non-injective Keller map of $\C^N$ with $h$ homogeneous, then $MC(SU(N'))$ is false for every $N'\ge N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The contrapositive of the implication gives $MC(SU(N))$ false. For $N'>N$ the map $G'=(G,x_{N+1},\dots,x_{N'})=x-h'$ with $h'=(h,0,\dots,0)$ has the same degree of homogeneity, with the zero polynomial taken as homogeneous of every degree, as the computations do. Its Jacobian determinant is $\det JG=1$ and it is non-injective, so the implication at $N'$ fails. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Poisson endomorphisms and padding]\label{prop:poisson}
Let $\Pi$ be the constant matrix of the brackets of the coordinates of $P_n$ and $F:\C^{2n}\to\C^{2n}$ polynomial. The substitution $g\mapsto g\circ F$ is a Poisson endomorphism of $P_n$ if and only if $JF\,\Pi\,JF^t=\Pi$. If it is one and $F$ is not injective, it is not an automorphism, and $F\times\mathrm{id}_2$ is a non-injective Poisson endomorphism of $P_{n+1}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the chain rule,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chain}
\{g\circ F,h\circ F\}=\sum_{j,k}(\partial_jg\circ F)(\partial_kh\circ F)\{F_j,F_k\},\qquad \{F_j,F_k\}=(JF\,\Pi\,JF^t)_{jk}.
\end{equation}
The substitution preserves the bracket for all $g,h$ exactly when the right side equals $\{g,h\}\circ F=\sum_{j,k}(\partial_jg\circ F)(\partial_kh\circ F)\Pi_{jk}$, that is when $\{F_j,F_k\}=\Pi_{jk}$ for all $j,k$ (take $g,h$ coordinates for the converse). An automorphism of $P_n$ has a polynomial inverse, so $F$ would be bijective. For the padding, the matrix $JF\times I_2$ is block diagonal and $\Pi$ extends by a canonical block, so the criterion holds, and points $(a,c),(b,c)$ with $F(a)=F(b)$ collide. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Smale's 16th problem]
The Jacobian conjecture is false for every $N \ge 3$ [D] by Proposition~\ref{prop:dummy}; the two-variable case remains open [C].

\begin{corollary}[Mathieu conjecture, 1997]\label{res:mathieu}
Mathieu proved that his conjecture, taken for all compact connected Lie groups, implies the Jacobian conjecture \cite{Mathieu1997} [U] (the original paper was not located; the implication is checked in Zwart's review, \cite[Theorems~4.16 and~4.23]{Zwart2025} [V]); Duistermaat and van der Kallen proved the abelian case \cite{DvdK1998} [U]. At a fixed $N$ the argument shows that the conjecture for $SU(N)$ makes every Keller map $x-h$ of $\C^N$ with $h$ homogeneous invertible \cite[Theorems~4.16 and~4.23]{Zwart2025}. De Bondt's homogenization of the counterexample \cite{deBondt2026}, re-implemented and verified in Section~\ref{sec:fifteen}, gives such a map on $\C^{15}$, of degree $7$, which is not injective [MV]; the cubic-homogeneous form on $\C^{24}$ of Section~\ref{sec:fortyeight} is another [MV]. By Proposition~\ref{prop:mathieu} the Mathieu conjecture is \textbf{false for $SU(N)$, $N \ge 15$} [D], after adjoining identity coordinates for $N > 15$. For $SU(2)$ an explicit counterexample is given in \cite{LongSU2} [V], and its identities hold exactly for $n\le\nsuthreemax$ [MV]. For $3 \le N \le 14$ the argument does not reach $F$: its components have total degrees $7, 6, 4$ and its second partial derivatives have no common zero, so $F$ is not affinely equivalent to a map $x - h$ with $h$ homogeneous [MV] by Proposition~\ref{prop:homtype}, and the reduction of $F$ to that form adds variables \cite{BCW1982}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Dixmier conjecture, 1968]
$JC(2n)$ implies $\mathrm{Dixmier}(n)$, and $\mathrm{Dixmier}(n)$ implies $JC(n)$ \cite{BKK2007, Tsuchimoto2005} [V]. Since $JC(n)$ is false for every $n \ge 3$, the second implication gives that $\mathrm{Dixmier}(n)$ is false for every $n \ge 3$ [D]: the \textbf{full Dixmier conjecture is false}. Ranks $1$ (Dixmier's original question) and $2$ remain open [C]; the first implication gives no information on rank $2$, because it runs from $JC(4)$ to $\mathrm{Dixmier}(2)$. A proof of rank $1$ is claimed in \cite{Zheglov2024} [U].
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Poisson conjecture, 2007]\label{res:poisson}
For each $n$, $JC(2n) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Poisson}(n) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Dixmier}(n) \Rightarrow JC(n)$ in any characteristic, where $\mathrm{Poisson}(n)$ concerns the canonical Poisson algebra in $2n$ variables \cite[Theorem~7]{AvdE2007} [V]. Hence $\mathrm{Poisson}(n)$ is false for every $n \ge 3$ [D]. Index $2$ is settled separately: Long gives explicit $R, T, D, S \in \Q[x,q,p,z]$ with $\{D,R\} = \{S,T\} = 1$ and the other brackets zero, where $R = x(2-3xq)$ is reducible, so $(x,q,p,z) \mapsto (R,T,D,S)$ is a Poisson endomorphism of the canonical Poisson algebra in four variables that is not an automorphism \cite{LongPoisson2026} [V], checked exactly in Section~\ref{sec:poissonresult} [MV]. Hence $\mathrm{Poisson}(n)$ is false for every $n \ge 2$ [D] by Proposition~\ref{prop:poisson}. Index $1$ remains open [C]; a false $\mathrm{Poisson}(2)$ gives no information on $\mathrm{Dixmier}(2)$, since the implication runs from $\mathrm{Poisson}(2)$ to $\mathrm{Dixmier}(2)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Gaussian moments conjecture]
$GMC(n)$ for all $n$ implies $JC$ \cite{DEZ2017} [V]; hence \textbf{GMC is false} at some finite dimension. An explicit counterexample in three variables, with $P$ of five terms and total degree $4$, is given in \cite{Long2026} [V], and its identities $E(P_3^m)=0$ and $E(Q_3P_3^m)=m!$ hold exactly for $m\le\ngmcmax$ [MV]; GMC is therefore false in every dimension $n \ge 3$ [V]. GMC holds in dimension $1$ \cite[Proposition~4.2]{DEZ2017} [V], and a proof of the two-variable case is claimed in \cite{Wilson2026} [U].
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Zhao's vanishing conjecture]
The vanishing conjecture for Hessian-nilpotent polynomials is equivalent to JC \cite{Zhao2004, Zhao2007} [V]; hence it is \textbf{false}: there exists a Hessian-nilpotent quartic $P$ with $\Delta^m P^m = 0$ for all $m$ but $\Delta^m P^{m+1} \ne 0$ for infinitely many $m$ [D]. An explicit witness is given in Section~\ref{sec:fortyeight} [MV].
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Image conjecture]
The Image conjecture implies the vanishing conjecture \cite{vdE2010} [U]; hence it is \textbf{false in some dimension} [D].
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Symmetric / gradient Jacobian conjecture]
JC reduces to cubic homogeneous maps with symmetric Jacobian \cite{dBvdE2005} [V]; with the Zhao equivalence this yields a dimension in which a \textbf{gradient Keller map is non-invertible} [D]. An explicit such map, in dimension 48, is given in Section~\ref{sec:fortyeight} [MV]. Smaller failing dimensions for the vanishing conjecture, $28$ for a non-homogeneous and $30$ for a homogeneous potential over the Gaussian integers, are reported in \cite{deBondt2026} [V]; they are not re-verified here.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{A homogeneous witness in dimension 15 (EXP-138)}\label{sec:fifteen}

De Bondt \cite{deBondt2026} starts from the volume-normalized form~\eqref{eq:normalised} of the counterexample, with $\det JF = 1$ and $F(0,1,-8) = F(-1,1,4)$, and reduces the degrees of its components with new variables until every component of the homogenized map has the form $x_i + H_i$ with $H_i$ homogeneous of degree $7$. Algorithm~A4 re-implements this construction independently and verifies the output exactly: $G = x + H$ on $\C^{15}$, $(JH)^{\nhomnil} = 0$, so $\det JG = 1$, and the distinct integer points
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pts15}
(0,1,-8,0,\dots,0,1)\quad\text{and}\quad(-1,1,4,-4,-1,48,12,-36,-12,32,8,-40,-12,12,1)
\end{equation}
have the same image [MV]. With $h = -H$ this is a non-invertible Keller map $x - h$ with $h$ homogeneous, which is what Result~\ref{res:mathieu} needs. The dimension-15 map lowers the threshold of the cubic form of Section~\ref{sec:fortyeight}, dimension 24, by nine. The smallest homogeneous counterexample known to the programme has dimension 15; a cubic homogeneous one of dimension 19 is reported by van Rijn [U], and the groups $SU(N)$ with $3\le N\le14$ stay undecided by this route. The verification is cheap, \nhomseconds\ s in all, because the nilpotency chain has only \nhomnil\ steps, so any reader who has the construction can repeat it. The result is a statement about one explicit map and not about a family: it gives the Mathieu failure from dimension 15 and does not by itself bound the smallest homogeneous counterexample from below.

\subsection{The explicit witness in dimension 48 (EXP-041)}\label{sec:fortyeight}

As of 2026-07-22 no explicit symmetric, gradient, or Hessian-nilpotent witness had been published (on a search of the literature; the July 2026 consequence notes are non-constructive in dimension). We construct one in three steps, each verified in exact arithmetic with algorithm~A3.

\emph{Input.} W. Thompson's explicit Bass--Connell--Wright cubic-homogeneous form of the counterexample \cite{Thompson2026}: $G = U + H$ on $\Q^{24}$, $H$ cubic homogeneous with \nthompsonmon\ monomials, with a published rational collision pair. We verified this form independently (homogeneity; the exact collision; nilpotency of $JH$ by an exact sparse chain). The nilpotency index is $\nnilindex$, not $17$ as stated there: $(JH)^{\nnilindex} = 0$ while $(JH)^{17}$ retains \njhseventeen\ terms [MV].

\emph{Construction} (de Bondt--van den Essen \cite{dBvdE2005}). The potential $f = -i \sum_{j=1}^{24} H_j(x + iy)\, y_j$ is a homogeneous quartic in 48 variables (\npotentialmon\ monomials over $\Q(i)$) with nilpotent Hessian (their characteristic-polynomial identity reduces this to the nilpotency of $JH$). The triangular conjugation~\eqref{eq:conj} was verified identically in all 48 components, so $\widetilde F = \mathrm{id} + \nabla f$ is a symmetric Keller map ($\det J\widetilde F = 1$) [MV].

\emph{The collision.} With $(u, v)$ Thompson's collision pair and $w = i\,(I + JH(v)^t)^{-1}(u - v)$, the points $p_1 = (u, 0)$ and $p_2 = (v - iw,\, w)$ are distinct and satisfy $\widetilde F(p_1) = \widetilde F(p_2)$ exactly over $\Q(i)$. \textbf{Hence the Hessian conjecture fails in dimension 48 with an explicit witness}, and $P_\star = -f$ is an explicit homogeneous Hessian-nilpotent quartic falsifying the vanishing conjecture [MV]. The finite certificate of failure is the collision itself; the consistency check $\Delta P_\star^2 \ne 0$, forced by Zhao's Corollary~3.9 for any failing potential, holds with \ndeltaterms\ terms [MV]. The reality obstruction is intrinsic: a nonzero real Hessian-nilpotent polynomial does not exist \cite{Zhao2004} [V], so the $\Q(i)$ coefficients are unavoidable. The symmetric positive results, dimension $\le 4$ nonhomogeneous and $\le 5$ homogeneous \cite{dBvdE2005b, vdEW2004} [U], leave the minimal witness dimension open between those bounds and the failing dimensions of \cite{deBondt2026}, 28 and 30 [V], and 48 for the witness checked here.

A bounded literature search of 2026-09-18 on Dixmier ranks 1 and 2, $GMC(2)$ and $SU(N)$ found Long's $SU(2)$ paper, Wilson's and Zheglov's preprints, and nothing on $SU(N)$ for $3\le N\le23$ at that date; it did not locate Long's rank-two Poisson paper, which a later reading of the recent literature supplied. No independent route computes powers of $h(f_H)$ itself. The consistency check $\Delta P_\star^2\ne0$ with \ndeltaterms\ terms is a necessary condition for a failing potential and is not a disproof by itself.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The status table is a table of indices. The same conjecture is true, false and open at different indices, and the reason is the direction of the implications. The implication $DC(n)\Rightarrow JC(n)$ carries the falsity of $JC(n)$ to every rank $n\ge3$. The implication $JC(2n)\Rightarrow DC(n)$ carries truth upward and silence downward: it would give $DC(1)$ from a proof of $JC(2)$ and $DC(2)$ from a proof of $JC(4)$, and a counterexample to $JC(4)$ gives nothing. The conditional closures make this explicit: a proof of $JC(2)$ would decide $DC(1)$ and $PC(1)$ true, and a counterexample to $JC(2)$ would decide $DC(2)$ false and $PC(2)$ false, which for $PC(2)$ is now already decided.

The Poisson row shows that the closure is a lower bound on what is known. The chain $JC(2n)\Rightarrow PC(n)\Rightarrow DC(n)\Rightarrow JC(n)$ leaves $PC(2)$ undecided, and Long's explicit map decides it by direct construction, with a fibre of three points over $(0,\tfrac18,0,0)$. An explicit Poisson endomorphism is stronger than the chain in one way and weaker in another. It is stronger because it gives a witness, which the chain never gives. It is weaker because $PC(2)$ false carries no information on $DC(2)$ or $JC(2)$, whose implications run out of $PC(2)$. The undecided entries of the table are therefore undecided by the located implications, not open in the literature, and the table says so in its open column.

The Mathieu row shows the cost of a reduction that adds variables. The located proof treats maps $x-h$ with $h$ homogeneous, the counterexample is not of that form (Proposition~\ref{prop:homtype}), and the Bass--Connell--Wright route to the homogeneous form multiplies the dimension. The failure for $SU(N)$ therefore starts where an explicit homogeneous map lives: 24 for Thompson's cubic form, 15 for de Bondt's degree-7 form. The statement that the Mathieu conjecture fails for $SU(3)$ would need a dimension-preserving passage from general Keller maps to homogeneous type, which the exposition does not supply; the located proof stops at Theorem~4.4, which adds variables. It is a negative result of this paper that the three-dimensional map alone does not reach $SU(3)$ through the located proofs; the original proof in Mathieu's paper might, and it was not located.

The three witnesses differ in what they certify. The dimension-48 map is a symmetric Keller map with an explicit collision and a Hessian-nilpotent potential with \npotentialmon\ monomials; de Bondt's failing dimensions 28 and 30 for the vanishing conjecture are smaller. The 48-dimensional witness is not minimal, and its value is that every step is verified exactly: the input digest, the conjugation, the collision and the potential. It also corrects the nilpotency index of the input from 17 to \nnilindex. The dimension-15 map is the only witness whose construction was re-implemented from the three-dimensional map; the dimension-48 construction takes Thompson's cubic form as an input that is verified and not re-derived. Long's Poisson map is a direct construction in four variables and the only witness here whose source formulas were transcribed from a PDF and checked against its printed sizes, which is why the transcription test and the two controls matter more for it than for the others.

The two negative results of the paper are the homogeneous-type obstruction of $F$ and the open range $3\le N\le14$ of the Mathieu row. Both are statements about located proofs: they leave open whether a non-invertible Keller map of homogeneous type exists in dimension below 15, which would lower the threshold, and a search for one is a stated next step.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} Every computation uses the same algebra system, sympy, for Gr\"obner bases, determinants and expansions; the second routes reduce but do not remove the risk of a library defect. The fibre of $F$ in \EXP{001} is read off one lex basis without the radical certificate that \EXP{144} adds. The formulas of Long's map were transcribed from the page images of one arXiv version (v1), and the transcription test of term counts and degrees, the Poisson identities and the controls make an undetected error implausible without excluding it. The Bass--Connell--Wright ladder from the three-dimensional map to Thompson's 24-variable form was not rerun, so that form is an input verified for homogeneity, collision and nilpotency and not a derived object. The version of the algebra system is recorded for \EXP{001} and \EXP{144} only; the sizes and times of the other runs are those of the archived artifacts. The time of the homogeneous-type test is not recorded.

\paragraph{External validity.} Mathieu's 1997 paper was not located, so the Mathieu row rests on Zwart's expository rendering of Theorems~5.3 and~5.4 at fixed $N$, marked [V], and on the original, marked [U]. If the original contains a dimension-preserving argument for general Keller maps, the counterexample would decide $SU(N)$ for $3\le N\le14$ and the row would change. Wilson's and Zheglov's proofs are preprints and are not checked here [U]. De Bondt's dimensions 28 and 30 are read in the primary text and not re-verified [V]. The page numbers of the located theorems were not recorded, except for the polynomials of Long's map, so the table gives theorem numbers and not pages for the other rows. The symmetric positive results for small dimensions are quoted as stated in the primary papers that cite them, whose full texts were not fetched [U].

\paragraph{Construct validity.} The marks separate what was computed from what was derived or read. The padding steps (dummy variables for $JC$, identity coordinates for Mathieu, identity canonical pairs for Poisson) are proved in Section~\ref{sec:theory} but not computed beyond the cases listed, so they are [D]. The closure was computed for $n\le\nclosuremax$ and the all-$n$ statement is [D]: the rules do not depend on $n$. The Markus--Yamabe conjecture and the factorial conjecture are excluded from the table because the implication direction of the located statements does not carry the failure of the Jacobian conjecture to them; for the Markus--Yamabe conjecture the date of an independent earlier refutation is recorded without a primary source [U].

\paragraph{Scope.} The paper covers seven conjectures and the indices that the located implications and the explicit witnesses decide. It does not resolve the two-variable Jacobian conjecture, the Dixmier conjecture at ranks 1 and 2, or the Poisson conjecture at index 1. It does not decide $SU(N)$ for $3\le N\le14$ or the other compact groups, and it gives no witness for the Image conjecture.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

The counterexample on $\C^3$ decides the seven neighbouring conjectures at specific indices only. The Dixmier and Poisson conjectures are false at every index $n\ge3$, the Poisson conjecture also at $n=2$ by Long's explicit map, checked exactly in \npoissonseconds\ s. The Mathieu conjecture is false for $SU(N)$ with $N\ge\nhomdim$ through a degree-\nhomdeg\ homogeneous map and for $SU(2)$ by Long's functions, and is not decided by the located proofs for $3\le N\le14$. The Gaussian moments conjecture is false for $n\ge3$. The vanishing, Hessian and symmetric forms fail with an explicit witness in dimension \nwitnessdim, a potential with \npotentialmon\ monomials and a collision over $\Q(i)$. Every check is exact and every negative control fires. The Mathieu row, the Dixmier ranks 1 and 2 and the Poisson index 1 are the rows whose open indices a later source or computation can change.

The next steps are a search for a non-invertible Keller map of homogeneous type in dimension below 15, which would lower the Mathieu threshold; an in-house check of Wilson's two-variable argument; and the location of Mathieu's 1997 paper, which would settle whether the counterexample reaches $SU(N)$ for $3\le N\le14$.
