\begin{abstract}
For a univariate integer polynomial $f$, let $\tau(f)$ be the minimum number of
$+,-,\times$ gates needed to compute $f$ from $x$ and the constants $-1,0,1$, and let
$z(f)$ be its number of distinct integer roots. The Shub--Smale tau conjecture
(Smale's fourth problem) asserts $z(f) \le (1+\tau(f))^{\kappa}$ for a universal
$\kappa$; it implies $\mathrm{P}_{\C} \ne \mathrm{NP}_{\C}$ in the Blum--Shub--Smale
model and $\mathrm{VP}^0 \ne \mathrm{VNP}^0$, and it is open even for $\kappa = 1$.
We report what is, to our knowledge, the first exact census of the conjecture's
growth function at the bottom of its ladder: writing $\zmax(\tau)$ for the maximum of $z(f)$ over all nonzero $f$ with
$\tau(f) \le \tau$, we prove by exhaustive, exactly verified computation that
\[
\zmax(1),\dots,\zmax(9) \;=\; 1,\, 2,\, 3,\, 3,\, 4,\, 5,\, 5,\, 6,\, 6 .
\]
In particular the minimum cost of $4$ distinct integer roots is $5$ gates, of $5$
roots is $6$ gates, and of $6$ roots is exactly $8$ gates, by an
explicit verified witness: $q(q-2)(q-6)$ for $q = x^2 - x$, computable in $8$ gates by
chained subtraction sharing; the growth function has plateaus at $\tau = 4$,
$\tau = 7$ and $\tau = 9$, so an extra gate does not always buy an extra root. No
program with at most nine gates computes a polynomial with $7$ distinct integer
roots, and ten gates suffice, so the seven-root threshold is exactly $10$. At the
ninth gate a multiplicative final gate is decided through unions of known root sets,
and an additive one by an exhaustive scan of all $4.19\times10^{12}$ candidates with
a $2$-adic and $3$-adic divisibility filter that is valid for roots anywhere in
$\Z$, so no evaluation window is involved.
The censuses are decision-complete: depth $6$ required the exact construction of
all $25{,}844{,}905$ reachable computation states; depth $7$ a complete scan of
$2{,}013{,}706$ new polynomials, using a ``last-gate'' lemma that decides one depth
beyond any exhausted frontier without storing the next frontier; and depth $8$
the first complete construction of the depth-$7$ frontier
itself: $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$ states, built out-of-core by hash-partitioned
external deduplication and scanned exhaustively ($\sim\!2 \times 10^{11}$
last-gate applications) by a validated multiprocess engine. The enumerator is
anchored on Markstr\"om's published exhaustive census of integer targets (all
fourteen anchor values reproduced exactly) and cross-checked against an independent
computer-algebra root counter on $284$ polynomials, and on a second, separately assembled
pool of $228$. The six-root threshold is
decided by a case split: the last gate of an $8$-gate $6$-rooter must involve the
seventh value, and if it is a multiplication its root count is a union of two known
root sets, so that case reduces to a co-occurrence query over the depth-$6$ frontier;
the scan found $408$ witnesses. We further prove two elementary
stall theorems explaining the observed record mechanisms: for any monic
$h \in \Z[x]$ of degree at least $2$, towers built by iterating $h$ alone have
depth-independent integer root counts (for $h = x^2 - 2$, the iterates $h^{\circ k}(x) - x$
keep exactly $2$ integer roots against $2^k$ real roots), and across the quadratic
family $h_c = x^2 - c$ with $c \le 200$ the maximum tower yield is $5$, attained only
at $c = 2$; a second yield series at $c = m^2 + m + 1$, produced by genuine integer
$2$-cycles, is identified and closed by the classical fact that integer polynomial
cycles have length at most $2$. All computations are exact integer arithmetic,
reproducible from the released code. We also report
the exact ladders of a digit-restricted census (a restricted form that Rojas relates
to the full conjecture): the maximum number of \emph{odd} roots grows as
$1,2,2,2,2,3,4$ through $\tau = 7$, with its own extremal family
$(x^2-1)(x^2-9)$, distinct from the unrestricted records.
Finally we place the census against its two neighbouring worlds, measured on the
same enumerated programs. Over $\mathbb{F}_p$ the analogous maximum is
\emph{exactly} $2^{\tau-1}$ for every $\tau$ (proved here; the extremal program is
$k$ squarings and one subtraction, and the Fermat primes that the small cases
suggest are not required, only a prime $p \equiv 1 \bmod 2^k$). Over $\mathbb{R}$ it
is $1,2,3,4,6,8$ through $\tau = 6$, exhaustively, by exact Sturm counting with no
floating point; its $\tau = 4$ and $\tau = 6$ records are linked by a step
$g \mapsto g\cdot(g-x^2)$ that costs two gates and doubles the real root count,
beating a Chebyshev tower's three gates per doubling, and that step itself stalls
after three iterations. The three ladders agree at $\tau \le 2$ and separate at $\tau = 3$
and $\tau = 4$ respectively; over $\mathbb{F}_p$ the degree ceiling $2^{\tau-1}$ is
saturated at every $\tau$, over $\mathbb{R}$ it is not, and over $\mathbb{Z}$ the
census stays linear. A five-gate program with six distinct \emph{real} roots and
only two integer ones exhibits the difference concretely.
The work is experimental mathematics in the tradition of Markstr\"om's census; it
decides nothing about the conjecture asymptotically.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The conjecture}\label{sec:conjecture}

A \emph{constant-free straight-line program} (SLP) over $\Z[x]$ is a sequence
$(1, x, f_2, \dots, f_N)$ in which every $f_i$ ($i \ge 2$) is a sum, difference, or
product of two earlier entries; following \cite{ShubSmale1995, Rojas2003} we write
$\tau(f)$ for the least $N - 1$ over all such programs with $f_N = f$. Equivalently
\cite{Burgisser2024}, $\tau(f)$ is the minimal number of operation gates of an
arithmetic circuit over the inputs $x$ and the free constants $-1, 0, 1$. Let $z(f)$
denote the number of distinct roots of $f$ in $\Z$.

\begin{conjecture}[Shub--Smale \cite{ShubSmale1995}; Smale's fourth problem \cite{Smale1998}]
There is a universal constant $\kappa \ge 1$ such that every nonzero
$f \in \Z[x]$ satisfies $z(f) \le (1 + \tau(f))^{\kappa}$.
\end{conjecture}

Evidence marks follow the statements below: \textbf{[V]} read on the primary source,
\textbf{[V2]} stated by a secondary source that was read, \textbf{[U]} unverified (the source
was read at abstract level only, or not read). The conjecture fails for $\kappa < 1$ by an
elementary count \emph{[D]}: the product $\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x - 2^i)$ has $N$ distinct integer
roots and costs at most $3N + 1$ gates (double the previous power of two, subtract it from
$x$, multiply into the running product), so $z(f) \ge (\tau(f) - 1)/3$ for a family of
programs of every length. Rojas is cited for the observation that geometric-progression
products acquire one root per $O(1)$ gates \cite{Rojas2003} [U as to the exact form of his
statement]. The conjecture is open even for $\kappa = 1$.

Its significance is structural. Shub and Smale proved that it implies
$\mathrm{P}_{\C} \ne \mathrm{NP}_{\C}$ in the Blum--Shub--Smale model
\cite{ShubSmale1995, BCSS1998} [V2, through \cite[\S4.6]{Burgisser2024}; the original paper
was not accessed, and its statement is taken from the survey's equation (4.5) and from
\cite{Rojas2003}]. In the form that Cheng reads from it, the implication is that if $n!$ is
ultimately hard, that is, no nonzero multiple of $n!$ has straight-line complexity
$(\log n)^c$, then the algebraic version of $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$ holds
\cite[\S1]{Cheng2004} [V]. B\"urgisser \cite{Burgisser2009}, building on Koiran
\cite{Koiran2004}, proved that the conjecture implies $\mathrm{VP}^0 \ne \mathrm{VNP}^0$, and
that hardness of the single sequence $(n!)$ already yields that separation [V2, as
Theorem 4.17 of \cite{Burgisser2024}; the primary paper was read at abstract level]. The
consequence for integer factoring is recorded in the survey literature: easy factorials would
give nonuniform polynomial-time integer factoring (the remark on p.~126 of
\cite{BCSS1998}, cited through \cite{Koiran2004}) [V2], and Lipton relates straight-line
complexity and factorization \cite{Lipton1994} [V2, through \cite[\S4.6]{Burgisser2024}; not
read directly]. With integer division allowed, $n!$ \emph{is} easy \cite{Shamir1979} [V2, as
presented in \cite{Koiran2004}], so the restriction to $+,-,\times$ carries the entire
content.

The upper-bound side for $n!$ itself is known unconditionally to be
$O(\sqrt{n}\,\log^2 n)$ ring operations over $\Z$, a bound that Cheng attributes to Strassen
and to the textbook of B\"urgisser, Clausen and Shokrollahi \cite[\S1]{Cheng2004} [V as
stated by Cheng; Strassen's paper was not read, [U]]. Cheng proves, conditionally on a
smooth-numbers-in-short-intervals conjecture, that a nonzero multiple of $n!$ is computed by a
program of length at most $\exp\bigl(c\sqrt{\log n\,\log\log n}\,\bigr)$ for an absolute
constant $c$ \cite[Thm.~1]{Cheng2004} [V]; this does not decide the polylogarithmic
question that the conjecture asks about. The real-zeros analogue of the conjecture is false:
iterating the logistic map produces $2^j$ real roots at cost $O(j)$ \cite[Example~1]{Rojas2003}
[V], which motivated Koiran's real tau conjecture for structured expressions (Conjecture~4.1
of \cite{Burgisser2024} [V2]; the supporting papers
\cite{Koiran2011, KPTT2015, Hrubes2013, BriquelBurgisser2020} were read at abstract level
[U], and \cite{Dutta2021} was not read [U]). On the lower-bound side, no nontrivial lower
bound on $\tau(n!)$ was known when Koiran wrote \cite{Koiran2004} [V]. On the reduction side,
Rojas proved that his $p$-adic Digit Conjecture implies the full statement (Theorem~1 of
\cite{Rojas2003}) and bounded the valuation spectrum by $s \le N_p(s) \le s(s+1)/2$,
independently of $p$ (Theorem~2) [V]; the exact statement of the digit conjecture is not
transcribed in our records, and Section~\ref{sec:digit} states which form we measure [U].
Recent work applies the conjecture to an explicit exponential lower bound
\cite{BBDM2026} and tightens the bridge from BSS separations to constant-free Valiant
separations \cite{Burgisser2026} [U, both at abstract level]; the current survey reference is
\cite[\S4.6]{Burgisser2024} [V].

\subsection{What this paper does}

The literature contains, to our knowledge, exactly one exhaustive computation in this
model: Markstr\"om's census of \emph{integer targets} (the straight-line complexity
of small factorials and primorials), complete to program length $11$ on 2013-era
hardware \cite{Markstrom2014}. We found no published computation of the
\emph{polynomial} side, i.e.\ the actual growth function of the conjecture: searches of
arXiv for the tau conjecture, for Shub--Smale with integer roots and for straight-line
programs with integer roots return nothing of this kind after 2019 [V]; priority itself
remains [U]. We define
\[
\zmax(\tau) \;=\; \max\{\, z(f) \;:\; f \in \Z[x] \setminus \{0\},\ \tau(f) \le \tau \,\}
\]
and determine it exactly for $\tau \le 9$, together with the full record galleries
through $\tau = 7$, explicit optimal witnesses, and the exact cost thresholds for
$3, 4, 5, 6$ and $7$ distinct integer roots. We also
prove two elementary theorems that
explain the observed record mechanisms and close entire construction classes
(Section~\ref{sec:stall}), and we measure the quadratic tower family exhaustively
(Section~\ref{sec:family}).

Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The census} (Section~\ref{sec:census}): for $\tau = 1,\dots,9$, the
values of $\zmax(\tau)$ are $1$, $2$, $3$, $3$, $4$, $5$, $5$, $6$, $6$,
decision-complete, with per-depth state and polynomial counts, record galleries
and replayed witness programs; plateaus occur at $\tau = 4$, $7$ and $9$.
\item \textbf{Exact thresholds}: the minimum $\tau$ admitting $3$ distinct integer
roots is $3$ ($x^3 - x$); for $4$ roots it is $5$; for $5$ roots it is $6$; for $6$
roots it is exactly $8$ (Section~\ref{sec:window}); for $7$ roots it is exactly $10$
(Section~\ref{sec:seven}: the complete depth-$8$ census excludes $8$, the two halves of
the ninth gate exclude $9$, and two $10$-gate witnesses exist).
\item \textbf{A method lemma} (Lemma~\ref{lem:lastgate}): every polynomial of cost
$d + 1$ is one gate over a normalized depth-$d$ state, so $\zmax(d+1)$ is computable
from an exhausted depth-$d$ frontier without storing the depth-$(d+1)$ frontier. This
bought depth $7$ (a $\sim$$10^9$-state frontier avoided).
\item \textbf{Stall theorems} (Section~\ref{sec:stall}): iterating any fixed monic
map of degree $\ge 2$ yields depth-independent integer root counts; for the
Chebyshev-conjugate map $x^2 - 2$, whose difference-of-squares splittings generate
all census records at depths $5$--$7$, the exact stable core is $\{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$.
\item \textbf{The family measurement} (Section~\ref{sec:family}): across
$h_c = x^2 - c$, $c \le 200$, and all tower shapes to four iterations, the maximum
integer-root yield is $5$, attained only at $c = 2$; two yield-$4$ series exist,
$c = m(m+1)$ (fixed and anti-fixed points) and $c = m^2 + m + 1$ (genuine
$2$-cycles), and the classical bound on integer polynomial cycle lengths shows this
inventory is complete.
\item \textbf{The seven-root threshold} (Section~\ref{sec:seven}): the upper bound
is verified by two structurally different $10$-gate programs, executed gate by
gate, one folding ($q(q-2)(q-6)\cdot(x-4)$, root set the interval
$\{-2,\dots,4\}$) and one squaring ($x^2(x^2-1)(x^2-4)(x^2-16)$, root set the
tower $\{0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm4\}$); the squaring shape generalizes to a family giving
$2m+3$ roots in $3m+4$ gates. On the lower side, no $9$-gate program whose final
gate is a \emph{multiplication} reaches $7$ roots, over all $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$
depth-$7$ states (maximum union size exactly $6$). For an \emph{additive} final
gate we prove a window-free divisibility criterion: if $f$ has $k$ distinct integer
roots, then on any block of $p^a$ consecutive integers the $p$-adic valuations of
the values of $f$, corrected for a proven power of $x$, must sum to at least a
number that depends only on which values vanish modulo $p^e$. Applied with $p = 2$
and $p = 3$ to every one of the $4.19\times10^{12}$ additive candidates over the
same states, with every survivor built and root-counted exactly, it finds no
seven-rooter. Hence $\zmax(9) = 6$ and the threshold is exactly $10$. An earlier
scan on the window $[-32,32]$, with a sharp confinement lemma pricing what the
window misses, is kept as an independent partial check.
\item \textbf{The three worlds} (Section~\ref{sec:fp}): on the same enumerated
programs, $\zpmax(\tau) = 2^{\tau-1}$ exactly for every $\tau$ [D], while
$\zrmax(1..6) = 1,2,3,4,6,8$ [MV] and the integer census stays linear. What limits
$\mathbb{Z}$ is not degree and not splitting, both of which cheap programs reach
at exponential scale, but \emph{distinctness}: five gates already give a
completely split polynomial of degree $16$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ with two distinct
roots.
\item \textbf{Anchoring and cross-checks} (Section~\ref{sec:anchor}): the enumerator
reproduces all fourteen of Markstr\"om's published anchor values; an independent
computer-algebra cross-check agrees on $284$ polynomials and on a second pool of $228$; and two general-purpose
solver engines, validated on known witnesses, do not complete the smallest
free-structure search within their time limits (Section~\ref{sec:depth8}).
\end{enumerate}

Every theorem-level result and every literature statement in this paper carries a mark:
\textbf{[MV]} machine-verified in exact arithmetic from the released code, \textbf{[D]}
proved here (elementary, self-contained), \textbf{[C]} conjectural or observational, and,
for statements about other authors' work, \textbf{[V]}, \textbf{[V2]} and \textbf{[U]} as
defined in Section~\ref{sec:conjecture}. Nothing here decides the conjecture in
either direction, and no asymptotic consequence is claimed: the census maps where the
conjecture lives at the bottom of its ladder, in the same genre as
\cite{Markstrom2014}.

\section{Model, normalization, and the census method}\label{sec:model}

\begin{definition}\label{def:model}
Programs operate on values in $\Z[x]$. The inputs are $-1$, $1$ and $x$; each gate
applies $+$, $-$ or $\times$ to two (not necessarily distinct) earlier values; the
length of a program is its number of gates; $\tau(f)$ is the minimal length of a
program computing $f$ at some gate.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[free $0$ is redundant]\label{lem:zero}
Adding $0$ as a fourth free input does not change $\tau$. \emph{[D]}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In any program using the input $0$: $a + 0$ and $a - 0$ duplicate $a$; $a \times 0$
computes $0$; $0 - a$ equals $(-1) \times a$, one gate either way. Replacing each use
and deleting duplicated gates never lengthens the program.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[normalization]\label{lem:norm}
Every $f$ has an optimal program in which no gate recomputes an input or an earlier
value, and no gate computes $0$. \emph{[D]}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A gate duplicating an available value can be deleted, rewiring its uses to the
original; a computed $0$ is usable only in the redundant patterns of
Lemma~\ref{lem:zero}. Each deletion shortens the program, so an optimal one contains
neither.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[reached-set sufficiency]\label{lem:state}
The extensions available to a program depend only on the \emph{set} of values it has
computed. Consequently a breadth-first search over reached sets (``states''), with
set-level deduplication, visits every normalized reachable state of each depth
exactly once. \emph{[D]}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $I = \{-1, 1, x\}$ for the inputs. A normalized program of length $d$ has $d$
distinct non-input, nonzero gate values; its \emph{state} is the set $S$ of those values.
The gate-$(d{+}1)$ values that keep a program normalized are
\[
  C(S) \;=\; \bigl\{\, a \circ b \;:\; a, b \in I \cup S,\ \circ \in \{+,-,\times\} \,\bigr\}
  \setminus \bigl( \{0\} \cup I \cup S \bigr),
\]
and $C(S)$ is a function of the set $S$ alone: a gate reads two earlier entries only through
their values, so neither the order of the gates nor the identity of the program that
computed $S$ enters. We show by induction on $d$ that the states of depth $d{+}1$ are exactly
the sets $S \cup \{v\}$ with $S \in \mathcal{F}_d$ and $v \in C(S)$, where $\mathcal{F}_d$
is the family of states of depth $d$ ($\mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset\}$). If $P$ is a
normalized program of length $d{+}1$, its first $d$ gates are a normalized program (a
gate that repeats an input, repeats an earlier value or computes $0$ would already
violate normalization), whose state $S$ lies in $\mathcal{F}_d$, and its last gate value
lies in $C(S)$. Conversely, for $S \in \mathcal{F}_d$ and $v \in C(S)$, any program of
state $S$ followed by the gate that produces $v$ is a normalized program of length
$d{+}1$ with state $S \cup \{v\}$. Hence a breadth-first search that starts from
$\{\emptyset\}$, expands each state by $C(S)$ and deduplicates the resulting sets reaches
every element of $\mathcal{F}_{d+1}$, and each exactly once after deduplication. Finally,
if $\tau(f) = d$, an optimal program is normalized (Lemma~\ref{lem:norm}), so $f$ belongs to
some state of $\mathcal{F}_d$, and to none of $\mathcal{F}_{d'}$ with $d' < d$ because
that would give a program of length $d' < \tau(f)$ computing $f$.
\end{proof}

This is the polynomial analogue of the range-isomorphism reduction of
\cite{Markstrom2014}. The census algorithm is exactly this BFS, in exact integer
arithmetic on dense coefficient tuples, with each new polynomial's first appearance
depth recorded (that depth is $\tau(f)$, by Lemmas~\ref{lem:norm} and
\ref{lem:state}). Integer roots are counted exactly: strip the power of $x$, then
test the divisors of the trailing coefficient (both signs). Algorithm~\ref{alg:bfs} gives
the procedure and Algorithm~\ref{alg:roots} the root count.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Reached-set census by breadth-first search, with exact first-appearance depths.}
\label{alg:bfs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require maximum depth $D$
\Ensure for each depth $d \le D$: $|\mathcal{F}_d|$ and the polynomials first seen at depth $d$
\State $\mathcal{F}_0 \gets \{\emptyset\}$; \ $\mathrm{first} \gets \{\}$ (a map from polynomial to depth)
\For{$d = 1, \dots, D$}
  \State $\mathcal{F}_d \gets \emptyset$
  \For{each state $S \in \mathcal{F}_{d-1}$}
    \State $O \gets I \cup S$ \Comment{$n = d + 2$ operands}
    \State $K \gets \{\, a + b,\ a \times b \;:\; \{a, b\} \subseteq O \text{ unordered, } a = b \text{ allowed} \,\} \,\cup\, \{\, a - b \;:\; (a, b) \in O^2,\ a \ne b \,\}$
    \For{each $v \in K$ with $v \ne 0$ and $v \notin O$}
      \State add the sorted tuple of $S \cup \{v\}$ to $\mathcal{F}_d$ \Comment{set-level deduplication}
      \If{$v \notin \mathrm{first}$} $\mathrm{first}[v] \gets d$ \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Exact count of distinct integer roots of a nonzero $f \in \Z[x]$.}
\label{alg:roots}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require coefficients $f_0, f_1, \dots, f_N$ with $f \ne 0$
\State $m \gets$ least index with $f_m \ne 0$; \ $R \gets \{0\}$ if $m > 0$, else $\emptyset$
\State $g \gets f / x^m$
\If{$\deg g \ge 1$}
  \For{each divisor $d$ of $|g_0|$ and each $r \in \{d, -d\}$}
    \If{$g(r) = 0$ (Horner, exact integers)} add $r$ to $R$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndIf
\State \Return $R$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Cost and memory of Algorithm~\ref{alg:bfs}.} A state of depth $d{-}1$ has
$n = d + 2$ operands (the three inputs and $d - 1$ values), and $K$ holds
$n(n{+}1)/2$ sums, $n(n{+}1)/2$ products and $n(n{-}1)$ differences, that is $2n^2$ gate
applications. Expanding the whole depth-$(d{-}1)$ frontier therefore costs
$2 n^2 \, |\mathcal{F}_{d-1}|$ polynomial operations, each quadratic in the degrees of its
operands for products and linear for sums [D]. For the expansion of the depth-$6$ frontier
into depth $7$ this is $2 \cdot 9^2 \cdot 25{,}844{,}905 = 4{,}186{,}874{,}610$
applications, of which $2{,}434{,}020{,}495$ yield a distinct nonzero new value
(the number of successor rows of the out-of-core build below). Memory is dominated by the
resident frontier $\mathcal{F}_d$ and by the interned polynomials: the in-memory
implementation reaches depth $6$ with $25{,}844{,}905$ states and a depth-$7$ census by
the last-gate lemma; the depth-$7$ frontier of $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$ states at $28$ bytes
each is $29.4$ GB as integer ids alone and is built out-of-core (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ooc}).
Measured run times of the in-memory stages on one core: depth $5$, $64$\,s; depth $6$
scan, $295$\,s (the witness reconstruction to $1005$\,s in total); the construction of the
depth-$6$ frontier and the scan that decides depth $7$, $86.5$ minutes ($17$ minutes for the
frontier, $69$ for the scan). The cost of Algorithm~\ref{alg:roots} is dominated by
factoring the trailing coefficient by trial division, $O(\sqrt{|g_0|})$ steps, followed by
$2\,\sigma_0(|g_0|)$ Horner evaluations, where $\sigma_0$ counts divisors; this is why the
tower shapes of Section~\ref{sec:family}, whose trailing coefficients reach $c^{2^k}$, use a
proved window instead.

\begin{lemma}[last gate]\label{lem:lastgate}
Let $\mathcal{F}_d$ be the set of normalized reached states of depth $d$. Every $f$
with $\tau(f) = d + 1$ is $a \circ b$ for a single gate $\circ$ and operands $a, b$
available in some state of $\mathcal{F}_d$. Hence
$\zmax(d+1) = \max\bigl(\zmax(d),\ \max z(a \circ b)\bigr)$ over one op over every
state of $\mathcal{F}_d$, and this maximum is computable without constructing
$\mathcal{F}_{d+1}$. \emph{[D]}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take an optimal program of length $d + 1$ for $f$ and normalize it
(Lemma~\ref{lem:norm}; deletions would shorten it, contradicting optimality, so it is
already normalized). Its first $d$ gates form a normalized reached state of depth
$d$, and its last gate is one op over that state's available values.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Closing the six-root window}\label{sec:window}

The six-root threshold is exactly $8$:

\begin{theorem}[MV, both directions]\label{thm:window}
There is an $8$-gate constant-free SLP whose output has $6$ distinct integer roots,
and none with $7$ gates. An explicit witness (replayed gate by gate in exact
arithmetic) is, with $q = x(x-1)$:
\[
x{-}1;\quad 2;\quad q = x\cdot(x{-}1);\quad 4;\quad q{-}2;\quad (q{-}2){-}4 = q{-}6;\quad
q(q{-}2);\quad f = q(q{-}2)\cdot(q{-}6),
\]
whose root set is $\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Hence $\zmax(8) \ge 6$.
\end{theorem}

The method is a case split. By Lemma~\ref{lem:lastgate} at depth $8$, an $8$-gate
$6$-rooter is one gate over a depth-$7$ state $S \cup \{v\}$; since
$\zmax(7) = 5$, the final gate must involve the seventh value $v$. If that gate is a
multiplication, $f = v \cdot b$ and $z(f) = |R_v \cup R_b|$: a pure co-occurrence
condition on known root sets, decided exactly by one scan of the $25{,}844{,}905$
depth-$6$ states: $408$ hits, three reconstructed as explicit
programs and verified independently of the scan machinery. A construction search over
program schemas, which builds the constants $\{2, 6\}$ independently, reaches this
witness family only at $9$ gates; the scan's witness builds $\{2, 4\}$ and reuses the
subtraction chain $q \mapsto q-2 \mapsto q-6$, saving one gate. The five-rooters with
$\tau \le 7$ do not share one root set: the $67$ five-rooters realize seven root-set
patterns, including the
non-consecutive $\{-1,0,1,2,4\}$ and $\{-4,-2,-1,0,1\}$, and it is precisely such
shifted and punctured sets that leave room for a co-occurring factor to complete six
roots.

\subsection{The depth-eight resolution}\label{sec:depth8}

Depth $8$ is decided completely:

\begin{theorem}[MV]\label{thm:depth8}
$\zmax(8) = 6$, and no $8$-gate program of \emph{any} final-gate shape computes a
polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots. Hence the seven-root threshold is
$9$ or $10$.
\end{theorem}

The lower side ($\zmax(8) \ge 6$) is Theorem~\ref{thm:window}. The upper side
required the whole depth-$7$ frontier: it was \emph{constructed}, for the first time,
as $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$ reached-set states (out-of-core: 2.43 billion raw
28-byte successor rows, hash-partitioned into 256 files that deduplicate
independently in memory), with an exact internal cross-anchor: the
build's count of new depth-$7$ polynomials, $2{,}013{,}706$, equals the value
computed independently by the depth-$7$ scan of Section~\ref{sec:census}. The
frontier was then scanned exhaustively by the last-gate lemma
($\sim\!2 \times 10^{11}$ gate applications): zero
results with $7$ or more distinct integer roots. Every pipeline stage was first
validated on a known answer: the identical machinery one level down reproduces the
depth-$6$ frontier and the $\zmax(7) = 5$ census exactly. For completeness of the
methods: two general-purpose solver engines (nonlinear
integer arithmetic and pure bit-vector SAT over a gate-evaluation encoding; the SAT lane
follows the template of the encodings of shortest linear straight-line programs over
$\mathrm{GF}(2)$ \cite{FuhsSchneiderKamp2010}, known to us at abstract level only [U])
were validated semantically on pinned witnesses, yet neither completed even
the smallest free-structure known-answer search (six gates, five roots) within
time limits of $30$ minutes and one hour respectively; at this problem's scale,
exhaustive construction beat solver search.

Algorithms~\ref{alg:ooc} and~\ref{alg:scan} state the two stages. Both operate on states
stored as rows of seven polynomial identifiers (sorted, $4$ bytes each, so a state is
$28$ bytes), where identifiers index one interned table of $2{,}161{,}049$ distinct
polynomials; equal sets of polynomials therefore give equal sorted rows, which is what lets
a row hash decide set equality.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Out-of-core construction of the depth-$7$ frontier by hash-partitioned deduplication.}
\label{alg:ooc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the depth-$6$ frontier $\mathcal{F}_6$, rebuilt in memory by Algorithm~\ref{alg:bfs} and checked against $25{,}844{,}905$ states; $256$ partition files; fixed multipliers $m_1, \dots, m_7$ drawn once from a seeded generator
\Ensure $256$ files of distinct sorted rows whose union is $\mathcal{F}_7$
\ForAll{$S \in \mathcal{F}_6$} \Comment{expansion}
  \State $K \gets$ the distinct nonzero values $v \notin I \cup S$ of the $162$ gate applications over $I \cup S$
  \For{each $v \in K$}
    \State $\mathrm{row} \gets$ sorted identifiers of $S \cup \{v\}$; \ $h \gets \bigl(\sum_i \mathrm{row}_i\, m_i\bigr) \bmod 256$
    \State append $\mathrm{row}$ to buffer $h$; \ flush the buffer to file $h$ when it holds $200{,}000$ rows
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\For{$h = 0, \dots, 255$} \Comment{deduplication, one partition at a time}
  \State read file $h$ as a matrix of $28$-byte rows; sort and keep the distinct rows; write them; delete the raw file
\EndFor
\State \Return the per-partition distinct counts and their sum
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Exhaustive last-gate scan of a stored frontier (one worker, one partition).}
\label{alg:scan}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require partition file $h$ of $\mathcal{F}_7$; threshold $k = 7$; the interned table
\Ensure the histogram of $z(t)$ over all gate applications, and the witnesses with $z \ge k$
\ForAll{rows $S$ of the partition}
  \State $O \gets I \cup S$ ($10$ operands)
  \For{each of the $200$ gate applications $t = a \circ b$ over $O$}
    \State $t \gets$ the cached value for the key $(\circ, a, b)$, computed exactly on a miss
    \If{$t \ne 0$} $z \gets |\mathrm{IntegerRoots}(t)|$ (memoized); increment $\mathrm{hist}[z]$; record a witness if $z \ge k$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State write the record of the partition atomically (temporary file, then rename)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The digit-restricted census}\label{sec:digit}

Rojas' $p$-adic Digit Conjecture implies the full conjecture (Theorem~1 of
\cite{Rojas2003}) [V]. We read it as a bound on a digit-restricted class of integer
roots, and we measure two such classes: the odd roots and the roots $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$.
The exact statement of the digit conjecture is not transcribed in our records, so that this
reading, and in particular the claim that bounding the roots $\equiv 1 \pmod p$ for one fixed
prime suffices, is [U]; the measured ladders below stand on their own as exact counts.
We measured the restricted growth functions exactly through
$\tau = 7$: the maximum number of odd roots is
$1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4$, and for roots $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$:
$1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3$. The digit-restricted world has its \emph{own} extremal family, e.g.
$(x^2 - 1)(x^2 - 9)$ at exactly $7$ gates with all four roots odd: symmetric
products over odd squares, distinct from the unrestricted records (which crowd
$\{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$ and are digit-poor). The restricted form, which Rojas relates to the
full conjecture, is thus measurably harder to grow \emph{and} mechanically independent at
the bottom of the ladder.

\section{Record mechanisms}\label{sec:mech}

The census records decompose into a small move inventory [MV for the instances;
observational as a classification]: linear factors $x - a$ for available constants
$a$; block translation at one gate per unit shift; multiplication by the input $x$
(one gate, adjoins $0$); and difference-of-squares splittings $B^2 - A^2 = (B-A)(B+A)$
at two gates given $A$ and $B$. The depth-$5$ record
$x^2 - (x^2 - 2)^2 = -(x{-}1)(x{+}1)(x{-}2)(x{+}2)$ is the fundamental instance: its
inner map $C(x) = x^2 - 2$ is the Chebyshev-conjugate doubling map
($h(z) = z + 1/z$ satisfies $h(z)^2 - 2 = h(z^2)$), i.e.\ the record is the integer
shadow of exactly the iteration that makes the \emph{real} analogue of the conjecture
false. Over $\Z$, that factory is sterile beyond one splitting, which the next
section proves.

\section{Stall theorems}\label{sec:stall}

\begin{lemma}[escape]\label{lem:escape}
Let $h = x^d + \sum_{i<d} a_i x^i \in \Z[x]$, $d \ge 2$, and
$R = 1 + \sum_{i<d} |a_i|$. If $|x| \ge R + 1$ then $|h(x)| \ge |x| + 1$. \emph{[D]}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\sum_{i<d} |a_i|\,|x|^i \le (R-1)|x|^{d-1}$ for $|x| \ge 1$, so
$|h(x)| \ge |x|^{d-1}(|x| - (R-1)) \ge 2|x|^{d-1} \ge 2|x| \ge |x| + 1$ for
$|x| \ge R + 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[monic stall; D]\label{thm:stall}
Fix a monic $h \in \Z[x]$ of degree $\ge 2$. There is a constant $Z(h)$ such that
for every $k \ge 1$, both $h^{\circ k}(x) - x$ and the difference-of-squares towers
$G_k = h^{\circ (k-1)}(x)^2 - h^{\circ k}(x)^2$ have at most $Z(h)$ distinct integer
roots. One may take $Z(h) = \#([-M, M] \cap \Z)$ where
$M = \max(R+1, \max |a|)$ over the integer solutions $a$ of $h(y) = \pm y$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Integer roots of $h^{\circ k}(x) - x$ are periodic points of $h$; by
Lemma~\ref{lem:escape} all integer periodic points lie in $[-R-1, R+1]$. For $G_k$,
factor $G_k = (h^{\circ(k-1)} - h^{\circ k})(h^{\circ(k-1)} + h^{\circ k})$: an
integer root $x$ has $y = h^{\circ(k-1)}(x)$ solving $h(y) = \pm y$, so $x$ lies in
the increasing union $\bigcup_j (h^{\circ j})^{-1}(A) \cap \Z$ for the finite set $A$
of such $y$. If $|x| > M$ then $|h^{\circ j}(x)| \ge |x| + j > \max_{a \in A} |a|$
for all $j$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:escape} and induction, so no element of the union
exceeds $M$ in absolute value; the union is contained in a fixed finite set and
stabilizes.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[the Chebyshev core; D, MV spot-checks]\label{prop:cheb}
For $C(x) = x^2 - 2$: the integer periodic points are exactly $\{2, -1\}$; for every
$k \ge 1$ the polynomial $C^{\circ k}(x) - x$ has exactly $2$ distinct integer roots
while having $2^k$ distinct real roots and $\tau \le 2k + 2$; and the towers $G_k$
have root set $\{\pm 1, \pm 2\}$ for $k = 1$ and exactly $\{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$ for
every $k \ge 2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Orbits: $0 \mapsto -2 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 2$ and $1 \mapsto -1 \mapsto -1$; by
Lemma~\ref{lem:escape} (with $R = 3$; direct check for $|x| \ge 3$) all other integer
orbits escape, so the only cycles are the fixed points $2$ and $-1$, giving the first
two claims (the real-root count is the classical Chebyshev conjugacy). For $G_k$:
$h(y) = y$ has integer solutions $\{2, -1\}$ and $h(y) = -y$ has $\{-2, 1\}$;
integer preimages are $C^{-1}(2) = \{\pm 2\}$, $C^{-1}(-1) = \{\pm 1\}$,
$C^{-1}(-2) = \{0\}$, $C^{-1}(1) = C^{-1}(0) = \emptyset$, so the preimage closure
of $\{\pm 1, \pm 2\}$ is $\{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$ and is stable.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:stall} closes a construction class: no family built by iterating one
fixed monic map can witness superpolynomial $z$ versus $\tau$; a refutation of the
conjecture, if one exists, must build fresh constants or mix maps, paying gates for
them. That is precisely the regime the census measures.

\section{The quadratic family}\label{sec:family}

We measured all tower shapes ($h^{\circ k}(x) - x$ and
$h^{\circ i}(x)^2 - h^{\circ j}(x)^2$, $0 \le i < j \le 4$) across
$h_c = x^2 - c$ for $1 \le c \le 200$, in exact arithmetic. Root finding here cannot
use trailing-coefficient divisors (those constants reach $c^{2^k} \sim 10^{18}$);
instead, by Lemma~\ref{lem:escape}, every integer root of such a shape lies in
$[-(c+1), c+1]$, and direct evaluation over that window is exact. The two finders
agree on every shape where both are feasible ($c \le 10$, $j \le 2$) [MV].

\begin{theorem}[family census; MV]\label{thm:family}
For $1 \le c \le 200$ and the shapes above: the maximum number of distinct integer
roots is $5$, attained only at $c = 2$ (the set $\{0, \pm1, \pm2\}$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:cheb}). Nonzero yields occur exactly on two series:
$c = m(m+1)$, yield $4$ for $m \ge 2$ with root set $\{\pm m, \pm(m+1)\}$ (from the
fixed points $\{m{+}1, -m\}$ and anti-fixed points $\{m, -m{-}1\}$), and
$c = m^2 + m + 1$, yield $4$ with the same root sets, produced by the genuine
$2$-cycles $m \mapsto -m-1 \mapsto m$ (for $c = 1$: yield $3$, root set
$\{0, \pm 1\}$, from the cycle $0 \leftrightarrow -1$).
\end{theorem}

The fixed-point series is therefore not the only series that yields roots. The second
series is, however, the last one available to this construction class:

\begin{proposition}[cycle ceiling; D, classical]\label{prop:cycles}
For any $f \in \Z[x]$, every cycle of $f$ in $\Z$ has length at most $2$.
Consequently fixed points, anti-fixed points and $2$-cycles are the complete
periodic inventory harvestable by tower shapes over any integer polynomial map.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For integers $a \ne b$, $(a - b) \mid (f(a) - f(b))$. Around a cycle
$a_0 \mapsto a_1 \mapsto \dots \mapsto a_r = a_0$ of distinct points, the
differences $d_i = a_{i+1} - a_i$ satisfy $d_i \mid d_{i+1}$ cyclically, so all
$|d_i|$ equal one value $d > 0$, and the cycle is a closed walk on the arithmetic
line $a_0 + d\,\Z$ with steps $\pm d$. If two consecutive steps have opposite signs
then $a_{i+2} = a_i$, which forces $r = 2$; if all steps have the same sign the walk
never closes. Hence $r \le 2$. The fact is classical in the polynomial-cycles
literature; the standard treatment is that of Narkiewicz \cite{Narkiewicz1995}, which
was not accessible to us [U as to the exact attribution], and the proof is included to keep
the paper self-contained.
\end{proof}

Yield per gate across the family is maximized at $c = 2$ and decreases in $c$ (the
yield is bounded by $5$ while building $c$ costs $\tau(c) \sim \log c$ gates,
measured exactly against our anchored integer census): within this family, the
``parameterized loophole'' left open by Theorem~\ref{thm:stall} is empty [MV for
$c \le 200$; C beyond].

\section{The seven-root threshold}\label{sec:seven}

Theorem~\ref{thm:depth8} left the threshold for $7$ distinct integer roots at
$9$ or $10$. This section closes the upper side constructively, decides the
multiplicative half of the ninth gate exhaustively, decides the additive half first
on an evaluation window and then without one, and concludes that the threshold is
exactly $10$.

\subsection{Two ten-gate witnesses}\label{sec:seven:upper}

We give two $10$-gate witnesses, written as explicit programs and executed gate by
gate in the same exact arithmetic as the census; they are structurally different,
and Section~\ref{sec:seven:parity} compares them.

\begin{proposition}[upper bound; MV]\label{prop:ten}
Each of the following constant-free programs has length $10$ and computes a
polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(A)] With $q = x^2 - x$: $\; p_A = q(q-2)(q-6)\cdot(x-4)$, root set the
interval $\{-2,\dots,4\}$, height $56$.
\item[(B)] $\; p_B = x^2(x^2-1)(x^2-4)(x^2-16)$, root set the tower
$\{0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm4\}$, height $84$.
\end{enumerate}
Hence the seven-root threshold is at most $10$.
\end{proposition}

Both gate counts are exact against the model's conventions because the programs
are run as data rather than counted by hand; neither is claimed to be optimal,
which is the business of the scan below. Witness (A) costs nothing extra for its
third factor, since $q - 6 = (q-2) - 4$ and the constant $4$ is already in the
state of the exhaustive $8$-gate six-rooter $q(q-2)(q-6)$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:window}; appending $(x-4)$ costs one subtraction and one
multiplication. Witness (B) is cheap for a different reason: each of its
constants is a single squaring past the previous one ($2 \to 4 \to 16$), so it
costs one gate, and each is a perfect square, so it contributes a
\emph{pair} of integer roots.

That second mechanism generalizes.

\begin{proposition}[square-tower family; MV for $m \le 4$]\label{prop:tower}
Let $t_1 = 2$ and $t_{i+1} = t_i^2$, and set
\[
  p_m(x) \;=\; x^2\,(x^2-1)\prod_{i=2}^{m+1}\bigl(x^2 - t_i\bigr).
\]
Then $p_m$ has $z = 2m+3$ distinct integer roots and is computed by
$\tau = 3m+4$ gates for $m \ge 1$ ($3$ gates when $m = 0$): three gates per two
roots.
\end{proposition}

The family is a useful calibration precisely because it is \emph{not} uniformly
optimal. At $m = 0$ it gives $3$ roots in $3$ gates and the census says the
minimum for $3$ roots is exactly $3$; at $m = 1$ it gives $5$ roots in $7$
gates where the census achieves $5$ roots in $6$. It therefore supplies upper
bounds only, and it is the exhaustive scan below, not the construction, that
decides optimality at seven.

\subsection{Two arithmetics of opposite parity}\label{sec:seven:parity}

Witnesses (A) and (B) exploit different mechanisms, and the mechanisms differ
in parity. Folding by $q = x^2-x$ identifies $x$ with $1-x$, so every value
with integer preimages contributes exactly two roots: folding natively produces
\emph{even} root counts, and an odd count costs an extra linear factor.
Squaring by $y = x^2$ has one self-paired value, $0$, whose preimage is the
single point $x = 0$: squaring natively produces \emph{odd} counts, $2m+3$.

Seven is odd, so the tower is native there and the interval needs its patch,
and the two come out equal at ten gates. Across the exhaustive range an
interval is always \emph{among} the minimal-gate record sets, though it is not
the only one: at $\tau = 5$ the four-root records include the interval
$\{-1,0,1,2\}$ and also the difference-of-squares set $\{\pm1,\pm2\}$, computed
by $x^2 - (x^2-2)^2$, which is not an interval [MV]. The census therefore ends
exactly where the two mechanisms tie, and never separates them.

Height does separate them. Witness (A) has height $56$ against (B)'s $84$, and
at six roots the interval record has height $15$ against $84$ for the
corresponding tower. This matches a direct measurement over the census
witnesses: the minimal height of a polynomial in our catalog with $z$ distinct
integer roots is $1, 1, 2, 4, 15$ for $z = 2,\dots,6$ [MV], attained by
intervals. Additively structured root sets are the height-cheap ones;
multiplicatively structured ones buy their constants at doubly exponential
magnitude. We record this because it constrains proof strategies: a lower-bound
argument tuned to spread, multiplicatively built root sets will be blind to
interval sets, and conversely.

\subsection{The ninth gate, multiplicative half}\label{sec:seven:mult}

By the last-gate lemma a $9$-gate program is one gate over a depth-$8$ state,
and since $\zmax(8) = 6$ the final gate of any $9$-gate seven-rooter must
involve the eighth value. When that gate is a multiplication, $f = v\cdot b$
and $z(f) = |R_v \cup R_b|$, so the question reduces to unions of root sets
already known from the frontier, with no polynomial arithmetic in the inner
loop.

\begin{theorem}[MV]\label{thm:nine-mult}
No $9$-gate constant-free program whose final gate is a multiplication computes
a polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots. Over all $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$
depth-$7$ states, every one-gate extension and every operand, the maximum union
size is exactly $6$.
\end{theorem}

The scan was validated before the full run: the same machinery run at threshold $6$
returns $793$ unions on a single partition, so it detects unions when they
exist. This half is unconditional; root-set unions require no evaluation
window, and the root sets are computed exactly by Algorithm~\ref{alg:roots}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Union scan for a multiplicative ninth gate (one worker, one partition).}
\label{alg:union}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require partition file of $\mathcal{F}_7$; threshold $k = 7$; the interned table
\Ensure the maximum union size $\max_u$ and the list of hits with $u \ge k$
\ForAll{rows $S$ of the partition}
  \State $O \gets I \cup S$; \ $R_b \gets \mathrm{IntegerRoots}(b)$ for every $b \in O$ (memoized by identifier); \ $z^\ast \gets \max_b |R_b|$
  \If{$z^\ast = 0$} \textbf{continue} \Comment{a union of size $k$ would need $z(t) \ge k > \zmax(8)$}
  \EndIf
  \For{each of the $200$ gate applications $t = a \circ b'$ over $O$ with $t \ne 0$}
    \State $R_t \gets \mathrm{IntegerRoots}(t)$ (memoized)
    \If{$|R_t| + z^\ast < k$} \textbf{continue} \Comment{no operand can complete the union}
    \EndIf
    \For{each $b \in O$}
      \State $u \gets |R_t \cup R_b|$; \ $\max_u \gets \max(\max_u, u)$; \ record a hit if $u \ge k$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State write the record of the partition atomically
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:union} is correct because for a multiplicative ninth gate
$f = t \cdot b$ the distinct integer roots of $f$ are exactly $R_t \cup R_b$; the two
prunes use only $|R_t \cup R_b| \le |R_t| + |R_b|$ and $\zmax(8) = 6$. Its cost per state is
the $200$ gate applications plus at most $200 \times 10$ set unions of sets of at most
six integers, with no polynomial arithmetic in the innermost loop. The scan took $13.1$\,h
on the same machine and the same $256$-way partitioning as Algorithm~\ref{alg:scan}; its
per-worker memory is the same interned table and caches (an operation cache of at most
$4$ million entries and a root-set memo of at most $3$ million).

\subsection{The additive half, and what ``windowed'' costs}\label{sec:seven:add}

An additive final gate admits no such reduction, since root sets do not compose
across addition. The instrument works with values instead: every operand and
extension is evaluated on the window $[-32,32]$ modulo a $31$-bit prime, and
$f = v \pm b$ vanishes at a point only if the residues agree, so counting
modular agreements never misses a witness; candidates reaching seven agreements
are promoted to exact construction and exact root counting. Two numerical
points matter. First, $61$-bit primes overflow \texttt{int64} under
modular multiplication, so the modulus is $31$-bit. Second, identically-zero
polynomials agree at every window point and would otherwise flood the
promotion stage. They are excluded, but only under a degree side-condition:
agreement at all $65$ window points is discarded when the candidate's degree
bound is below $65$, since a nonzero polynomial of degree below $65$ cannot
vanish at $65$ points, and is \emph{retained} when the degree bound is $65$ or
more. The side-condition matters, because a $9$-gate polynomial can have degree
up to $2^8$, and discarding full agreement unconditionally would throw
away exactly the high-degree witnesses the instrument is designed to find.

An empty result from this instrument is \emph{windowed}: on its own it excludes
only witnesses all of whose roots lie in $[-32,32]$, and a $9$-gate program
can build constants as large as $2^{256}$ by repeated squaring. The following turns
that caveat from an unknown into a decidable criterion.

\begin{lemma}[confinement; D, with the bound MV]\label{lem:confine}
Let $f \in \Z[x]$ be nonzero with distinct integer roots $r_1,\dots,r_k$. Then
$\prod_i (n - r_i)$ divides $f(n)$ for every $n \in \Z$. Consequently, if $f$
has $7$ distinct integer roots and $c$ denotes its trailing (first nonzero)
coefficient, then every integer root of $f$ lies in $[-32,32]$ whenever
$|c| \le 395$, and, if $0$ is not a root of $f$, whenever $|c| \le 1187$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The $r_i$ are distinct integers, so $\prod_i (x - r_i)$ is monic in $\Z[x]$ and
divides $f$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$; by Gauss the cofactor lies in $\Z[x]$, and evaluating at
$n$ gives the divisibility. For the bound, suppose some root satisfies
$|r_j| \ge 33$. If $0$ is not a root then $c = f(0)$ and $|c| \ge \prod_i|r_i|$;
the six remaining roots are distinct and nonzero, so their product has absolute
value at least $|(-1)(1)(-2)(2)(-3)(3)| = 36$, giving $|c| \ge 33 \cdot 36 =
1188$. If $0$ is a root, write $f = x^m g$ with $c = g(0)$; the six other roots
are distinct and nonzero, and the five non-escaping ones give a product of
absolute value at least $12$, so $|c| \ge 33 \cdot 12 = 396$. An escaping root
therefore forces $|c| \ge 1188$ when $0$ is not a root and $|c| \ge 396$ in every
case, contradicting either hypothesis.
\end{proof}

The constant $395$ is sharp: searching seven-root sets containing an escaping root
returns minimum trailing coefficient exactly $396$, at
$\{-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,33\}$ [MV]. We note that the two witnesses of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ten} have trailing coefficients $48$ and $-64$, an order
of magnitude inside the criterion: the shapes that actually reach seven roots
cheaply are exactly the ones the window sees.

Lemma~\ref{lem:confine} does not make the windowed instrument unconditional. It
prices the gap, and we measured the price. Constructing every candidate
$f = t \pm b$ exactly on a sample of $6{,}400{,}000$ candidates drawn from four
hash-partitioned frontier files, and reading each one's true trailing
coefficient [MV] (Table~\ref{tab:window}); the last three percentages are of the
$6{,}306{,}102$ nonzero candidates.

exhaustive counts; the scan of the next theorem is exhaustive.

\begin{theorem}[MV, windowed]\label{thm:nine-add}
No $9$-gate constant-free program whose final gate is an addition or a subtraction
computes a polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots that all lie in $[-32,32]$.
Over all $1{,}048{,}460{,}912$ depth-$7$ states, every one-gate extension, every
operand and both signs, $1{,}272{,}725$ candidates reach seven agreements on the
window, and none of them has $7$ distinct integer roots.
\end{theorem}

The scan was gated before production: at threshold $5$ on $20{,}000$ states of one
partition it returns $41$ witnesses, each verified exactly, so it finds witnesses
when they exist. Its $256$ partition records each match the state count of the
corresponding frontier file in the frontier's SHA-256 manifest, so no partition was
scanned partially.

Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:nine-mult}, $\zmax(8) = 6$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:confine}, a $9$-gate program computing a polynomial with $7$
distinct integer roots, if one exists, has an additive final gate and a root
outside $[-32,32]$, hence a trailing coefficient with $|c| \ge 396$, and
$|c| \ge 1188$ when $0$ is not a root. The seven-root threshold is therefore $10$
unless this residual class contains a seven-rooter; strictly, it remains in
$\{9, 10\}$ on the strength of this scan alone. The next subsection decides it.

\subsection{The additive half without a window}\label{sec:seven:free}

The confinement lemma bounds where a missed witness could be; the following
criterion removes the window altogether. It uses the same divisibility, read
$p$-adically on a block of consecutive integers rather than archimedeanly at $0$.

\begin{lemma}[$p$-adic block criterion; D]\label{lem:padic}
Let $f \in \Z[x]$ be nonzero with distinct integer roots $r_1,\dots,r_k$, let
$x^m$ be the exact power of $x$ dividing $f$, and let $0 \le m' \le m$. For every
prime $p$ and every integer $n$ with $f(n) \ne 0$,
\[
v_p\bigl(f(n)\bigr) - \max(m'-1,0)\,v_p(n) \;\ge\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} v_p(n - r_i).
\]
Let $B$ be a block of $p^a$ consecutive integers and $e \ge 1$, and let $Z$ be the
set of $n \in B$ with $f(n) \equiv 0 \bmod p^e$. Put $w(t) = v_p(t)$ if $p^a \nmid t$
and $w(t) = a$ otherwise, and
\[
C(z) = \sum_{n \in B\setminus Z} v_p(n - z)\ (z \in Z),\qquad
M = \min_{\rho \bmod p^a}\ \sum_{n \in B\setminus Z} w(n-\rho).
\]
If $L_k(Z)$ is the sum of the $k$ smallest numbers in the multiset
$\{C(z) : z \in Z\} \cup \{M,\dots,M\}$ ($k$ copies of $M$), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
S(f) := \sum_{n \in B\setminus Z}\Bigl(v_p\bigl(f(n)\bigr) - \max(m'-1,0)\,v_p(n)\Bigr) \;\ge\; L_k(Z).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:confine}, $f = g\prod_i(x - r_i)$ with $g \in \Z[x]$. If
$m \ge 1$ then $0$ is one of the $r_i$ and the remaining factor $x^{m-1}$ divides
$g$, which gives the first inequality; for $m' = 0$ nothing is subtracted. On
$B \setminus Z$ the value $f(n)$ is nonzero, so the inequality holds there; sum it.
A root cannot lie in $B \setminus Z$. A root $z \in Z$ contributes exactly
$C(z)$. A root $r \notin B$ contributes at least $\sum_{n}w(n - \rho)$ with
$\rho = r \bmod p^a$, because $v_p(n - r) = w(n-\rho)$ when $p^a \nmid n-r$ and
$v_p(n-r) \ge a$ otherwise; distinct roots outside $B$ may share a residue, so each
contributes at least $M$. The least total over all placements of $k$ distinct roots
is $L_k(Z)$.
\end{proof}

The criterion is cheap because residues are exact: $f(n) \bmod p^e$ is computed
from the residues of the operands, and $L_k(Z)$ depends only on the zero mask of
the block, so it is tabulated once ($2^{16}$ masks for $p = 2$, $B = \{-8,\dots,7\}$,
$e = 64$, where unsigned $64$-bit arithmetic is exactly arithmetic mod $2^{64}$;
$2^9$ masks for $p = 3$, $B = \{-4,\dots,4\}$, $e = 20$). The power of $x$ is
bounded below by the operands' trailing indices (their sum for a product, their
minimum otherwise). Without that correction, candidates of the form $x^j h$ or
$2^j h$ pass the $2$-adic block easily; the $3$-adic block is blind to powers of
$2$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Construction of the bound table $L_k$ for one prime $p$, block $B$ of $p^a$ points and exponent $e$.}
\label{alg:table}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $p$, $a$, $e$, $k$; the points $n_0 < \dots < n_{L-1}$ of $B$ with $L = p^a$
\State $V[i][j] \gets v_p(n_j - n_i)$ for $i \ne j$ \Comment{valuation of the root--point differences inside $B$}
\State $W[\rho][j] \gets a$ if $L \mid (n_j - \rho)$ else $v_p(n_j - \rho)$, for $\rho = 0, \dots, L-1$
\ForAll{masks $Z \subseteq \{0, \dots, L-1\}$ (bit $i$ set when the value at $n_i$ vanishes modulo $p^e$)}
  \State $C_i \gets \sum_{j \notin Z} V[i][j]$ for $i \in Z$
  \State $M \gets \min_\rho \sum_{j \notin Z} W[\rho][j]$
  \State $L_k[Z] \gets$ the sum of the $k$ smallest numbers of $\{C_i : i \in Z\} \cup \{M, \dots, M\}$ ($k$ copies of $M$)
\EndFor
\State \Return the table $L_k$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{The block filter for every candidate $f = t \pm b$ of one batch of frontier states.}
\label{alg:filter}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require a batch of states; residue tables of the interned polynomials at the $16$ points of the $2$-adic block (mod $2^{64}$) and at the $9$ points of the $3$-adic block (mod $3^{20}$); degree and trailing-index tables; $k = 7$
\ForAll{states $S$, eighth gates $t = a \circ c$ over $O = I \cup S$ ($200$ of them), operands $b \in O$}
  \State $\deg t$ exactly for a product, at most the larger operand degree otherwise; $m' \gets$ sum (product) or minimum (otherwise) of the operands' trailing indices, then minimum with the trailing index of $b$
  \If{$\max(\deg t, \deg b) < k$} count as dropped by degree; \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \ForAll{signs $\sigma \in \{+, -\}$}
    \State $F_2 \gets$ residues of $t \pm b$ at the $2$-adic points (word operations on the stored residues)
    \If{the $2$-adic bound fails: $S_2(F_2, m') < L_k^{(2)}[\mathrm{mask}(F_2)]$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
    \State $F_3 \gets$ residues of $t \pm b$ at the $3$-adic points
    \If{the $3$-adic bound fails} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
    \If{$F_2 = 0$ and $F_3 = 0$ at every point} \Comment{suspect}
      \State build $f$ exactly; drop the candidate if the exact build is the empty polynomial
    \Else
      \State build $f$ exactly; \ \textbf{if} the build is the empty polynomial \textbf{then} count it as a zero eighth gate and drop it
    \EndIf
    \State $R \gets \mathrm{IntegerRoots}(f)$; \ report a hit if $|R| \ge k$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter}, $S_p(F, m') = \sum_{n \notin Z} \bigl( v_p(F(n)) - \max(m'-1,0)\,v_p(n) \bigr)$ is the left-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:block} read from the residues, and the exact build returns the empty polynomial in exactly two situations: the eighth gate $t$ is the zero polynomial (the build then stops at $t$, so $f = \pm b$ is not formed), or $f$ is identically zero.

\textbf{Costs and memory.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:table} runs once per prime: $2^{16}$ masks over $16$ points for $p = 2$ and $2^{9}$ masks over $9$ points for $p = 3$, with $O(2^L L^2)$ operations for the sums and a sort of $L + k$ numbers per mask; the tables hold $2^{16}$ and $2^{9}$ small integers ($256$ KiB and $2$ KiB). In Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter} the work per candidate that survives the degree test is $O(L)$ word operations, $16$ for the $2$-adic layer and $9$ for the $3$-adic layer, plus a table lookup; the degree test alone removes $96.0\%$ of the candidates. The residue tables of the $2{,}161{,}049$ interned polynomials occupy $2{,}161{,}049 \times 16 \times 8 = 276{,}614{,}272$ bytes ($2$-adic) and $2{,}161{,}049 \times 9 \times 8 = 155{,}595{,}528$ bytes ($3$-adic) [D from the table shapes], are memory-mapped and shared by the worker processes, and so is the packed polynomial store; states are processed in batches of $256$. The whole scan evaluated $4{,}193{,}843{,}648{,}000$ candidates in $21.4$\,h of wall-clock time on $20$ processes ($381$ process-hours), an aggregate $5.4 \times 10^{7}$ candidates per second and $3.1 \times 10^{6}$ per process-second [D from those two figures]. The expensive step is the exact build: $2{,}937{,}472{,}943$ candidates pass both layers, and $2{,}345{,}860{,}018$ of them are root-counted by Algorithm~\ref{alg:roots}.

computes a polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[MV]\label{cor:nine}
$\zmax(9) = 6$, and the minimum number of gates of a constant-free program
computing a polynomial with $7$ distinct integer roots is exactly $10$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By the last-gate lemma a $9$-gate program is one gate over a depth-$8$ state, and
since $\zmax(8) = 6$ a seven-rooter's final gate uses the eighth value; it is
multiplicative or additive, excluded by Theorems~\ref{thm:nine-mult}
and~\ref{thm:nine-add-free}. Hence $\zmax(9) \le 6$, and $\zmax(9) \ge \zmax(8) = 6$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:ten} gives the upper side of the threshold.
\end{proof}

\section{The three worlds, measured}\label{sec:fp}

It is folklore that the conjecture's analogue fails over finite fields, and
that $x^{p-1}-1$ is the reason; this is why the statement is always made in
characteristic zero. We record here that the finite-field question has an
exact answer, obtained on the \emph{same} enumerated programs as the integer
census, so that the two ladders are comparable term by term.

For a prime $p$ write $z_p(f)$ for the number of distinct roots of $f$ in
$\mathbb{F}_p$, defined only when $f \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$, and set
\[
  \zpmax(\tau) \;=\; \max\bigl\{\, z_p(f) \;:\; \tau(f) \le \tau,\; p \text{ prime},\;
  f \not\equiv 0 \!\!\pmod p \,\bigr\}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[D, with both halves MV]\label{prop:fp}
$\zpmax(\tau) = 2^{\tau-1}$ for every $\tau \ge 1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Upper bound.} For a value $v$ of a constant-free program let $\mu(v)$
count the \emph{distinct} multiplicative gates in its sub-DAG. We claim
$\deg v \le 2^{\mu(v)}$, by induction. The input has degree $1$ with
$\mu = 0$, and constants have degree $0$. For an additive gate $v = a \pm b$,
the sub-DAG of $v$ contains those of $a$ and $b$, so
$\mu(v) \ge \max(\mu(a),\mu(b))$ and
$\deg v \le \max(\deg a, \deg b) \le 2^{\max(\mu(a),\mu(b))} \le 2^{\mu(v)}$.
For a multiplicative gate $v = ab$ we have
$\mu(v) = |M(a) \cup M(b)| + 1 \ge \max(\mu(a),\mu(b)) + 1$, where $M(\cdot)$
denotes the set of multiplicative gates in a sub-DAG, and hence
\[
  \deg v = \deg a + \deg b \le 2^{\mu(a)} + 2^{\mu(b)}
  \le 2^{\max(\mu(a),\mu(b))+1} \le 2^{\mu(v)} .
\]
Counting \emph{distinct} gates, so that shared subexpressions are not charged
twice, is what makes the multiplicative step close: bounding the exponent by
$\mu(a)+\mu(b)+1$ instead would exceed $\mu(v)$ whenever $a$ and $b$ share
gates.

Now a sub-DAG with no additive gate computes only $\pm x^k$ or $0$, since the
free constants are $-1,0,1$ and products of those remain in $\{-1,0,1\}$; such
a polynomial has at most one distinct root in any field. So if $f$ is not of
that form its sub-DAG contains an additive gate, whence
$\mu(f) \le \tau(f) - 1$ and $\deg f \le 2^{\tau-1}$. Finally a polynomial
that is nonzero over a field has at most its degree many roots, and reduction
mod $p$ can only lower the degree.

\emph{Lower bound.} By Dirichlet there is a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod{2^k}$ for
every $k$. Modulo such $p$ the polynomial $x^{2^k} - 1$ has exactly
$\gcd(2^k, p-1) = 2^k$ roots, and it is computed by $k$ squarings and one
subtraction, so $\tau \le k+1$. Taking $k = \tau - 1$ gives $2^{\tau-1}$.
\end{proof}

Both halves were checked mechanically: the degree bound holds on every
non-monomial in the complete census to depth $6$, where it is moreover
attained exactly ($\max \deg = 2^{\tau-1}$ at each $\tau$, against $2^{\tau}$
for monomials), and the
construction was verified for $k = 1,\dots,8$ at
$p = 3, 5, 17, 17, 97, 193, 257, 257$ with root counts
$2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256$ [MV]. We note that the Fermat primes, which
the small cases suggest, are not needed: only $p \equiv 1 \pmod{2^k}$ is.

\subsection{The real ladder}\label{sec:real}

The real analogue of the conjecture is known to fail. We measured how far, on
the same enumerated programs, counting distinct real roots exactly: take the
square-free part $f/\gcd(f,f')$ and count real roots by Sturm's theorem in
exact rational arithmetic, with no floating point at any stage. The method
was first validated on seven known answers, among them $x^2+1$ (none), $x^2$ (a
double root counted once), $T_4$ (four), and $(x^2-1)(x^2-4)(x^2-16)$ (six).

and Figure~\ref{fig:ladders} give the three ladders on the same programs, with the rational
ladder of Section~\ref{sec:rational} as the fourth.

The real record at $\tau = 5$ is worth writing out, because it is produced by
the same difference-of-squares mechanism that Section~\ref{sec:mech} identifies
as the integer record-maker:
\[
  a = x\cdot x,\quad b = a - 1,\quad c = b \cdot b,\quad d = c - a,\quad
  e = b \cdot d,
\]
\[
  e \;=\; (x-1)(x+1)(x^2-x-1)(x^2+x-1).
\]
Five gates, \emph{six} distinct real roots $\pm 1, \pm\varphi, \pm\varphi^{-1}$
with $\varphi$ the golden ratio, and exactly \emph{two} distinct integer roots
[MV]. The program is cheap and the roots are there; four of them are simply
irrational. This is the conjecture's content in one line: what limits
$\mathbb{Z}$ is arithmetic, not the cost of manufacturing roots. It also shows
the real ladder is not merely the Chebyshev ladder: that tower reaches
$T_2 = 2x^2-1$ with two real roots at three gates and needs six for $T_4$'s
four, so at five gates it is still at two, against six for the $\tau = 5$ record [MV].

The $\tau = 6$ record exposes the mechanism. It is
\[
  x^8 - 7x^6 + 14x^4 - 7x^2 + 1 \;=\; (x^4-3x^2+1)(x^4-4x^2+1),
\]
whose left factor is exactly the $\tau = 4$ record $g_1 = (x^2-1)^2 - x^2$. So
the step from four gates to six is $g \mapsto g\cdot(g-x^2)$, costing
\emph{two} gates because $x^2$ is already in the state, and \emph{doubling} the
real root count. It beats the obvious construction: a Chebyshev tower costs
three gates per doubling ($T_2 = 2x^2-1$ is three gates and composition repeats
it), so it reaches only about $2^{\tau/3}$. And it is not merely a good
construction here, it matches the exhaustive census at both $\tau = 4$ and
$\tau = 6$.

It also stalls, in the same manner as the integer-side towers of
Section~\ref{sec:stall}. Iterating the step gives distinct real roots against
degree (Table~\ref{tab:doubling}) [MV]: three clean doublings, reaching $16$ distinct real roots at $8$ gates,
after which the iterate ceases to be totally real and the successive ratios fall
to $1.75$ and $1.71$.

We claim no rate for $\zrmax$: six points cannot distinguish

One structural point is worth recording. The $\tau = 6$ real record has
\emph{zero} integer roots, while the $\tau = 6$ integer record has five: at
equal cost the two worlds are optimized by entirely disjoint polynomials. The
real ladder is not the integer ladder with further roots attached, but a
different extremal problem that happens to share the difference-of-squares
mechanism at the bottom.

Of the three ladders, the $\mathbb{F}_p$ row is known exactly for every $\tau$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:fp}).

$\mathbb{F}_p$ the count doubles with every gate, which is the fastest the
degree bound permits, and the extremal programs are the cheapest available:
repeated squaring, then subtract one. The same polynomial $x^{2^k}-1$ has
exactly two integer roots, $\pm 1$, for every $k$.

The two ladders coincide: allowing denominators buys nothing at all. This is
not because the rational world is a copy of the integer one, and the extremal
objects are in fact different. The $\tau = 6$ rational record is
\[
  8x^6 - 10x^4 + 2x^2 \;=\; 2x^2(x-1)(x+1)(2x-1)(2x+1),
\]
with five distinct rational roots $\{0, \pm\tfrac12, \pm 1\}$ but only
\emph{three} integer ones, while the $\tau = 6$ integer record attains five
integer roots outright. A six-gate program for it, with the value of each gate, is
\[
\begin{gathered}
  g_1 = x\cdot x,\quad g_2 = g_1 + g_1 = 2x^2,\quad g_3 = g_2 - 1 = 2x^2 - 1,\\
  g_4 = g_3\cdot g_3,\quad g_5 = g_4 - g_1 = 4x^4 - 5x^2 + 1,\quad
  g_6 = g_2\cdot g_5 ,
\end{gathered}
\]
and $g_6 = 2x^2(4x^4 - 5x^2 + 1) = 2x^2(4x^2-1)(x^2-1)$ is the record [MV: the six gates are replayed in exact integer arithmetic by
\path{scripts/replay_rational_tau6.py}, and the command and its output are stored in
\path{context/2026-11-14-rational-tau6-program.md}, both in the problem folder of the repository]. The replay shows that
the program computes the record, so $\tau \le 6$ for it; that $\tau$ is not smaller, and that no
polynomial of at most six gates has more than five rational roots, is the classification of the
rational census, not of the replay. Half-integer
roots are cheap here, since the doubled square $2x^2$ is a single gate, and they still do not
raise the ceiling.

So integrality is not where the difficulty lives, at least at the bottom of the
ladder: the same growth function appears over $\mathbb{Q}$. Whatever separates
$\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{F}_p$ in the table above already
separates $\mathbb{Q}$ from them too. We state this as a measurement over the
census range and claim nothing asymptotic from it.

The mechanism is the one the three-worlds reading predicts. In
$\mathbb{F}_p^{\times}$, cyclic of order $p-1$, the equation $x^d = 1$ has
$\gcd(d,p-1)$ solutions, up to $d$ of them; in $\mathbb{Z}^{\times} =
\{\pm 1\}$ it has at most two, for every $d$. The exponential ladder is
precisely the $2^k$-torsion that the cyclic group carries and the integers do
not. In this light the conjecture asserts that no program shape recovers over
$\mathbb{Z}$ what the cyclotomic one achieves over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and the
census is consistent with that: through $\tau = 8$ nothing over $\mathbb{Z}$
does better than linear growth. What Proposition~\ref{prop:fp} contributes is
that the comparison is exact on one side, so the gap being conjectured is
a gap between a proved exponential and a measured linear rate rather than
between two informal impressions.

\section{Limits, and what would count as progress}\label{sec:limits}

Nothing above bears on the conjecture asymptotically: censuses to depth $8$ cannot
distinguish polynomial from superpolynomial growth, and our theorems close
construction classes rather than open the bounding problem. In the measured
landscape, every mechanism observed or excluded so far pays $\Theta(1)$
gates per root, the growth function's first structure (plateaus at $4$, $7$ and $9$) is
the visible cost of constant-building, and the geometric-progression family remains
the best known factory, linear-rate, which is what makes the conjecture fail for
$\kappa < 1$ (Section~\ref{sec:conjecture}; the attribution of the observation to
\cite{Rojas2003} is [U]).

The census makes the following open items precise:
(i) $\zmax(10)$ and the eight-root threshold, which lies in $\{10,\dots,13\}$ by
the square-tower family ($m = 3$ gives $9$ roots in $13$ gates) and the census;
the block criterion of Lemma~\ref{lem:padic} applies unchanged at the tenth gate,
but the depth-$8$ frontier it would need has not been built;
(ii) extend the integer frontier past Markstr\"om's length $11$ \cite{Markstrom2014},
including his monotonicity question for $\tau(n!)$;
(iii) measure the growth of the valuation-spectrum bound
$s \le N_p(s) \le s(s+1)/2$ of \cite{Rojas2003} [V for the bound; that the true rate
is open is [U]];
(iv) the census's $2$-adic instrumentation records that all depth-$5$--$7$ record
polynomials concentrate their roots on the valuations $\{0, 1\}$ [MV], consistent
with the pressure sitting on the digit-conjecture side of Rojas' reduction [C].

\section*{Data and code availability}

{\raggedright
All code, run artifacts, experiment records and the problem wiki are available at
\url{https://github.com/fsantibanezleal/CAOS_RESEARCH} under
\path{problems/computation-complexity/tau-conjecture/}, together with the package
\texttt{tclib} and its test suite. The computations are archived there as the
experiment records \texttt{EXP-001}--\texttt{EXP-015} (folder \path{experiments/});
each record holds the code, the artifacts and the result.\par}

Every root count reported here is exact, and the results that rest on an evaluation
window are identified. The census enumerator and all integer root counting
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:roots}) are exact integer arithmetic in the Python standard library.
Which stage uses what:
\begin{itemize}
\item The census to depth $7$, the depth-$8$ last-gate scan (Algorithm~\ref{alg:scan}) and
the multiplicative nine-gate union scan (Algorithm~\ref{alg:union}) count roots exactly
for every candidate; none of them has a window, and none uses a modular pre-filter. NumPy
serves only to store and read the frontier files as integer arrays.
\item The additive nine-gate scan of Theorem~\ref{thm:nine-add} is the only windowed
instrument: it evaluates operands on $[-32,32]$ modulo a $31$-bit prime as a necessary
condition, and promotes the survivors to exact root counting. Its result is stated for roots
inside the window.
\item The window-free additive scan of Theorem~\ref{thm:nine-add-free} uses NumPy for the
$2$-adic and $3$-adic residues of Lemma~\ref{lem:padic} and for the table lookups, and
builds and root-counts exactly every candidate that survives; the filter can over-admit but
never discards a candidate that has seven distinct integer roots (Lemma~\ref{lem:padic}).
\item The real and rational ladders and the computer-algebra cross-check use SymPy for
square-free decomposition with Sturm sequences, for the rational root theorem and for
factorization, in exact rational arithmetic with no floating point at any stage.
\end{itemize}
The interpreter of the census, depth-$5$, depth-$6$ and depth-$7$ runs was Python $3.13.0$.
The versions of Python for the later runs and of NumPy and SymPy for every run are not
recorded in the experiment records [U]; the window-free engine calls
\texttt{numpy.bitwise\_count}, so it needs a NumPy release that provides it, and its
residue arithmetic relies on unsigned $64$-bit wrap-around being exact modulo $2^{64}$. The
code that produced each result is pinned by repository commit: the enumeration core
\texttt{tclib/enum.py} at \texttt{0b1cb207}, the out-of-core pipeline at \texttt{46469d43},
the depth-$8$ scan at \texttt{b038bc2f}, the multiplicative nine-gate scan at
\texttt{9a5c726c}, the window-free additive scan with its filter library
\texttt{tclib/padic.py} at \texttt{d25ae268}, and its production engine at
\texttt{78b2bfa8} (all in the repository of the first paragraph).
