## 1. Charges and the Gauss law `[D]`

Let `P` be the Petersen graph, `Cut(P)` its cut space in `F_2^{E(P)}` and `Q = F_2^{E(P)} / Cut(P)`.
For a cubic graph `G` and any map `s : E(G) -> E(P)`, the **charge** of a vertex `v` is
`q(v) = [1_s(e1) + 1_s(e2) + 1_s(e3)]`, the class of the sum of the indicators of its three labels.

- **Good is neutral.** `v` is good if and only if `q(v) = 0`. If `v` is good its labels form a star,
  a cut. Conversely, labels `x, x, x` or `x, x, y` give a vector of weight 1, never a cut (`P` is
  bridgeless); three distinct labels give a vector of weight 3, which is a cut only if it is a star
  (`P` is 3-edge-connected and cyclically 5-edge-connected, so its 3-edge cuts are stars).
- **Gauss law.** For every vertex set `S`, `sum_{v in S} q(v) = [sum_{e in d(S)} 1_s(e)]`: the
  charge in a region equals the flux through its boundary, because an edge with both ends in `S`
  is counted twice. With `S = V(G)` the total charge is zero: this is the parity theorem (one
  charge alone is impossible).
- **Defect.** The Petersen defect `pd(G)` is the least number of charges of a map. A Petersen
  coloring is a charge-free configuration; a counterexample is a graph where neutrality cannot be
  reached without charges, the way a frustrated spin system has no zero-energy state. arXiv:2608.10028v4
  points to the zero-temperature spin-model reading of edge colorings (Beaudin, Ellis-Monaghan,
  Pangborn, Shrock 2010; Jaeger 1992); the charges are the excitations of that reading.

## 2. The charge space is the coset space of the cut code `[MV]`

`Cut(P)` is a binary linear code of length 15 and dimension 9 (the cut code, minimum weight 3);
`Q` has 64 elements, the cosets. Coset-leader weights: 0 (1 coset), 1 (15), 2 (45), 3 (3), so the
covering radius is 3. Under `Aut(P)` (order 120) the cosets split into six orbits:

| orbit | size | leader weight | leaders | pairs of labels landing here | label triples at a vertex landing here |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `0` | 1 | 0 | empty | equal labels | the 10 stars |
| `E` | 15 | 1 | one edge | the 30 pairs at line distance 1 | `x,x,y`, `x,x,x`, and 60 distinct triples |
| `D3` | 15 | 2 | two edges at distance 3 (unique leader) | the 15 pairs at distance 3 | 120 distinct triples |
| `D2` | 30 | 2 | two edges at distance 2 (two leaders, completing a 4-edge cut around an edge) | the 60 pairs at distance 2 | 240 distinct triples |
| `T1` | 1 | 3 | 5 triples of pairwise distance-3 edges (a partition of `E(P)`) | none | 5 distinct triples |
| `T2` | 2 | 3 | each with 10 triples of pairwise distance-2 edges | none | 20 distinct triples |

The distance lemma of `2026-09-19-conduction.md` is the statement that pairs land in `0, E, D3,
D2` according to their distance. The 1,326 stored pair witnesses of `G52` carry charges `E`
(1,279), `D2` (37), `D3` (8), `T2` (2) and never `T1` (probe of 2026-09-18, relabelled here).

## 3. Multipoles conduct classes `[D]`

A `k`-pole whose vertices are all good, with its dangling ends split into two connectors `C1`,
`C2`, has `[C1] = [C2]` (Gauss law with the charge-free region). The **conducted set** `D(M)` is the
set of classes `[C1]` over all charge-free maps; it is invariant under `Aut(P)`, so it is a union of
the six orbits. Alternating rings of two multipoles with disjoint conducted sets carry a charge in
at least half of their blocks (the ring theorem, `2026-09-19-conduction.md`, Theorem 4; the proof
uses only the Gauss law and holds for connectors of any size).

## 4. Restoration for 6-poles `[D]`

Let `G` have no Petersen coloring and `u, w` be non-adjacent vertices. For the 6-pole `G - u - w`
with connectors the three ends at `u` and the three ends at `w`, `0` is not conducted: class `0` at
`u` means the three labels form a star, so restoring `u` makes it good, and the Gauss law gives class
`0` at `w`, so `w` is restorable too, a Petersen coloring of `G`. In words: a critical pair `{u, w}`
always carries two equal nonzero charges, and `D(G - u - w)` is exactly the set of charge classes
that the pair can carry.

## 5. The route to Problem 11 (cyclically 5-edge-connected counterexamples), and its obstacle

A ring of 6-poles joined along 3-edge connectors has 6-edge cuts between arcs, so it can be
cyclically 5-edge-connected if its blocks are (no cycle-separating cut of size at most 4 inside a
block or through one junction). Two cyclically 5-edge-connected 6-poles with disjoint conducted
sets would give, by the ring theorem, cyclically 5-edge-connected cubic graphs of Petersen defect
at least `t` for every `t`, hence counterexamples in the class of Problem 11 of arXiv:2608.10028v4
and unbounded defect there.

The obstacle is visible at once: if both blocks are `H - u - w` with `H` Petersen colorable, both
conduct `0`, so the sets meet. Every known counterexample contains the Petersen 4-pole `W` with its
4-edge cut, which survives in any 6-pole cut from it. A disjoint pair therefore needs either 6-poles
of another shape (for instance `H - e1 - e2 - e3` with the ends of the three deleted edges as
connectors, where restoring the edges is not forced by equal classes), or a 6-pole with a small
conducted set cut from a graph that is itself cyclically 5-edge-connected. EXP-013 measures the
conducted sets of such 6-poles before any construction is attempted.

## 6. What EXP-013 measured: the core `{0, E, D2}` `[MV]`

Every 6-pole measured by EXP-013 and cut from a Petersen-colorable cyclically 5-edge-connected
graph (`P`, `J5`, `J7`, the dodecahedron; shape (a) `H - u - w` with the natural split, shape (b)
`H - e1 - e2 - e3` with all ten splits for `P`, `J5` and the dodecahedron) conducts `0`, `E` and
`D2`. The conducted sets that occur are the six unions of the core `{0, E, D2}` with subsets of
`{D3, T1, T2}` listed in the EXP-013 verdict. So the disjointness condition of section 5 never
holds between two such blocks, and a ring of them can fail to be colorable only through structure
finer than charges. Section 7 describes that structure exactly.

## 7. Transfer relations and their semigroups `[D]`

A **block** is a 6-pole `M` with its dangling ends split into an ordered left connector `L` and an
ordered right connector `R`. Its **transfer relation** is
`T_M = {(phi(L), phi(R)) : phi a map of M into E(P) with every vertex good}`, a boolean matrix on
`E(P)^3`. A ring of blocks `M_1, ..., M_t` joins end `j` of `R_i` to end `pi_i(j)` of `L_{i+1}`
(indices mod `t`). Every vertex of the ring lies in exactly one block, so a Petersen coloring of the
ring is the same as a choice of labels on the `3t` junction edges that every block accepts: the
ring is colorable if and only if the boolean product `T_1 Pi_1 T_2 Pi_2 ... T_t Pi_t` has a nonzero
diagonal entry, where `Pi_i` is the permutation matrix of the junction.

**Proposition 7.1 (block diagonal form).** `T_M` is contained in the union over the 64 classes `q`
of `S_q x S_q`, where `S_q` is the set of ordered label triples of class `q`; every junction matrix
maps each `S_q` onto itself. Hence every product of blocks and junctions is block diagonal over the
64 classes. *Proof.* The block is charge-free, so the Gauss law gives `[phi(L)] = [phi(R)]`; the class
of a triple is the sum of its three labels, which does not depend on their order.

**Proposition 7.2 (one sector per orbit).** For `alpha` in `Aut(P)`, `(x, y)` is in `T_M` if and
only if `(alpha x, alpha y)` is; the action of `alpha` on labels commutes with junction permutations,
which act on positions. So the diagonal block of a product over `alpha(q)` is conjugate to its block
over `q`, with the same trace. A ring is colorable if and only if, for some orbit `o` among
`0, E, D3, D2, T1, T2`, the product of the **sector matrices** (the blocks over a fixed
representative class of `o`) has a nonzero diagonal. The sector sizes are 60, 67, 48, 48, 30, 60
(check: `60 + 15*67 + 15*48 + 30*48 + 30 + 2*60 = 3375 = 15^3`).

**Corollary 7.3 (every ring length at once).** For a finite family of blocks, the products of at
least two generators (block, orientation, junction permutation) form a finite semigroup `Sigma` of
six-tuples of boolean matrices. Every ring of the family with at least two blocks is Petersen
colorable if and only if every element of `Sigma` has a nonzero diagonal in some sector, and `Sigma`
is computed by closing the set of products of two generators under right multiplication by
generators. The closed set, with the generators, is a certificate that anyone can re-verify by
products alone.

**Relation with charges.** `D(M)` is the set of sectors in which `T_M` is not zero; the ring theorem
of section 3 is the case where a product vanishes identically in every sector. The semigroup sees
more: a sector product can be nonzero and still have a zero diagonal. The charge sectors play the
part of superselection sectors, and the six sector matrices are the transfer matrices of a
one-dimensional vertex model whose ring partition function, over the boolean semiring, is the
colorability of the ring.

**Soundness of computed matrices.** Every computed entry is a solver model checked from the
definition, so a computed sector matrix can only miss entries. Missing entries can only lower
traces, so a positive trace computed from the matrices proves colorability; a zero trace is a
candidate to be decided on the explicit graph by a proof-checked solver run (EXP-014).
